## **1 Executive Summary** 

This work examines how microgravity conditions on spacecraft change the development of smoke aerosol relative to smoke on Earth. We perform simulations of particle formation and growth with the goal of capturing smoke dynamics similar to those realized in experiments performed aboard the International Space Station. The simulations provide detailed information about fluid/thermal/particle interactions that cannot be acquired experimentally on orbit. A surrogate material, dibutyl pthalate, is expelled from a hot surface in a rectangular chamber while ambient air is blown through the domain, convecting the developing smoke downstream. The study considers two flow rates, two geometric configurations, and the isolated effects of gravitational particle settling and thermophoresis. In Earth gravity, buoyancy produces asymmetries in all fields of interest and amplifies vapor-to-particle conversion near room temperature surfaces at the upper boundary of the simulation domain. Gravitational settling removes large particles from the aerosol, limiting the maximum particle size to _∼_ 40 _µ_ m and the mode particle size to _∼_ 5 _µ_ m. Without buoyancy or settling, the microgravity smoke plume is spatially symmetric and contains very large particles ( _>_ 40 _µ_ m). A bimodal particle size distribution results in microgravity when prominent recirculation occurs behind the hot vapor source. The distribution is comprised of large particles ( _>_ 20 _µ_ m) associated with long residency time, vapor rich, high temperature recirculation, and smaller particles ( _<_ 10 _µ_ m) that originate farther downstream of the hot vapor source. A distinct large particle mode does not appear in microgravity flows that do not have prominent high temperature recirculation, although the aerosol still contains large particles. Without recirculation, the mode particle diameter in microgravity is _∼_ 10 _µ_ m, or approximately twice that of terrestrial smoke. Results show that future experiments should carefully consider and quantify surface interactions and the presence of hot, recirculating vapor, in order to control geometry-dominated features of the particle size distribution. 

A manuscript based on this work will be submitted to the journal Aerosol Science & Technology. The final document, when accepted, will be provided to NASA.

## **2 Introduction** 

Gravity affects the evolution of aerosols by introducing buoyancy and particle settling. Buoyant convection modifies the time-temperature history of the aerosol, as well as the spatial distribution of particles, condensing vapor, and thermal energy. Gravity also causes the settling of large particles out of the aerosol, provided the chemistry and vapor concentrations allow for the generation of large particles with appreciable settling velocities. These factors combine to increase particle residency times in high temperature, vapor rich regions in microgravity systems relative to terrestrial or Earth gravity systems (Urban et al., 1998, 2005). As a result, smoke particles produced in microgravity tend to be larger than those produced on Earth (Ku et al., 1995, Urban et al., 1998). Measurements suggest microgravity smoke particles to be 2 _−_ 8 times larger than terrestrial smoke particles (Urban et al., 2005). Moreover, large particles (not only smoke particles) persist in microgravity, with mass concentrations of particles with diameter _>_ 100 _µ_ m measured at ten-times that of a nominal terrestrial environment (Liu et al., 1991). Total particulate mass concentrations are also more than five times greater in microgravity (Liu et al., 1991). Understanding gravitational effects is of practical interest because it assists in the design of mission-critical smoke detection systems for use in microgravity environments, including the International Space Station (Mulholland et al., 2015); systems developed and calibrated for terrestrial use may not perform correctly in microgravity because of inappropriate particle size sensitivities (Urban et al., 2005). 

The present work uses numerical simulation to explore the influence of gravity on pyrolysis smoke growth from a fundamental perspective. We simulate the formation and growth of dibutyl-phthalate (DBP) particles in Earth gravity and microgravity environments. We utilize direct numerical simulation via a Navier-Stokes nodal approach. The smoke originates from pyrolysis vapor expelled from a heated sample in a geometry similar to that considered in the SAME experiments (Mulholland et al., 2015). The intention of this effort is to quantify the differences between microgravity and Earth gravity smoke, and to identify the fluid dynamic mechanisms that cause the differences. This study considers two crossflow velocities of sufficiently low speed that buoyancy cannot be neglected in the presence of gravity. Simulations are presented to assess the dependence of particle size distributions on thermophoresis, particle settling, and flow geometry. The paper is organized as follows: we introduce the simulation and modeling methodology, followed by a description of the flow geometry and the parameter space under consideration; we then examine differences in the wake geometry attributable to buoyancy for the lower of two flow speeds; next, we present and discuss instantaneous scalar fields as a means to describe particle accumulation and growth in the buoyant and non-buoyant wakes that form downstream of the hot vapor source; after developing an understanding of how the particle fields interact with the fluid dynamics for the low speed cases, we consider the effects of thermophoresis, particle settling, flow geometry, and the higher flow speed; we conclude with a discussion of the results, summarize findings relevant to future experimentation, and outline directions for future work.

## **4 Results** 

### **4.1 FLOW CONFIGURATION** 

We simulate the formation and growth of DBP particles in air flowing over a heated sample that expels DBP vapor. The simulation mimics the smoldering or pre-conflagration stage of a fire studied in experiments performed aboard the ISS (Mulholland et al., 2015). A schematic of the computational domain is shown in Fig. 1. Room temperature air enters a long chamber and flows over a heated rectangular sample. Vapor released from the sample nucleates into particles that grow and are subsequently collected downstream for analysis. The flow is laminar and steady, allowing the two-dimensional representation to present physical characteristics largely representative of the SAME experiments (Mulholland et al., 2015). DBP is used as the surrogate material because its properties are known and its use in modeling is well-established (unlike the materials used in the SAME experiments).

### **4.4 PHYSICAL ASSUMPTIONS** 

All assumptions utilized in this work are listed below. 

- From the perspective of thermal conductivity and viscosity, the condensing vapor phase is considered sufficiently dilute such that _µ_ and _k_ are computed for air as a function of temperature and density (Kadoya et al., 1985). Both the air and the condensing vapor are considered calorically perfect gases with constant properties defined in Table 1. 

- The Mach number _M_ is only important for flows with _M_ ≳ 0 _._ 3. Although the physics are insensitive to variation in Mach number when the Mach number is small, the ratio of sound speed to convective speed imposes a large computational burden. The computational challenge is often remedied with a Boussinesq approximation, but this approach is not valid in the presence of large density variations. Additionally, an elliptical solve for pressure is computationally intractable for the number of iterations required to resolve the particle time scales. We instead use a scaled dynamic pressure as the reference pressure _Po_ = _ρo_ ( _c_ 1 _Uo_ )<sup>2</sup> , such that _P_<sup>_⋆_</sup> =<sup>_<u>ρα</u>_</sup> _P_<sup>_R_</sup> _o_<sup>_<u>αT</u>_</sup> , where _c_ 1 is specified to reduce the effective speed of sound to 8 m _/_ s. The flow field was found to be independent of _c_ 1 for the values used. 

- All particles are assumed to be spherical. 

- Condensed phase DBP properties are defined by their continuum values. 

- There is no momentum exchange between the particles and the fluid (the air/DBP vapor mixture); the particle velocity equals the fluid velocity, augmented by the thermophoretic velocity and the stokes settling velocity. The stokes settling velocity is assumed to apply for all particles in the domain (including the large particles). 

- The fluid and particles are in thermal equilibrium and have the same temperature. 

- Particle diffusion coefficients decrease rapidly with diameter. This poses a numerical challenge due to the lack of a physical mechanism for diffusing numerical errors. For this reason, the minimum diffusion coefficient considered in the particle mass fraction transport equations corresponds to the fifth particle size node – any node greater than the fifth node is assigned the particle diffusion coefficient of the fifth node. 

- The mass flux of DPB from the heated rectangle is spatially uniform and all surfaces have spatially uniform temperature. 

- Particles that impact a solid surface “stick” with 100% efficiency, and are removed from the flow. 

- The aerosol does not achieve a rigorous steady-state because particle evolution continues (slowly) in regions of the flow with recirculation, particularly behind the hot vapor source. The recirculation is characterized by high temperatures, long particle residency times, and the continuous introduction of additional vapor. The hydrodynamic and thermal fields, conversely, become steady after only a few seconds. Although a rigorous steady-state does not exist, all aerosol features develop and effectively stabilize shortly after the hydrodynamic fields. We therefore present all results at time _t_ = 7 seconds, which is considered to be the effective steady-state. At his time, all fields are either steady, or changing at a rate that does not affect the conclusions drawn from the simulations.

## **5 Summary** 

We have simulated smoke formation and growth in microgravity and Earth gravity to examine how buoyancy and particle settling affect thermal/fluid/particle interactions and the resultant smoke particle size distributions. The simulated geometry emulates the SAME experiments performed aboard the International Space Station (Mulholland et al., 2015). Dibutyl pthalate (DBP) vapor is released from a heated rectangular sample, which serves as a surrogate for the smoldering material used in the SAME experiments. 

Large particles ( _>_ 30 _µ_ m) are present in the wake region downstream of the heated vapor source, and large particle number densities ( _∼_ 10<sup>10</sup> m<sup>_−_3</sup> ) occur inside the wake vortices. The wake contains large particle number densities because dividing streamlines prevent the particles from being convected away, and the particles continue to grow as condensation consumes all available DBP vapor. The particles simultaneously grow via coagulation. Downstream of the wake, two parallel particle streams contain high number densities of both large and small particles. No particles are present far downstream with diameter _<_ 400 nm because nucleation only occurs for a short distance downstream of the heated vapor source. The particle size distributions contain local maximums near dp = 5 _µ_ m and dp = 35 _µ_ m for both flow speeds. The faster flow conditions present a slightly larger wake but this does not manifest as a meaningful difference in particle size distribution. Thermophoresis prevents particles from contacting the heated surface, which has been demonstrated by turning off the thermophoresis mechanism. Without thermophoresis, particles contact the heated vapor source and undergo evaporation. With respect to the particle size distribution, this has the effect of decreasing the number of large particles and increasing the number of small particles – this supports an assertion that the wake is responsible for producing the largest particles in the system. 

When gravity is present, all fields become asymmetric due to buoyant convection. Upward convection in the faster flow brings DBP vapor into close proximity with the cool upper surface, resulting in locally high nucleation rates near the upper wake vortex and the upper surface. Large particle number densities ( _∼_ 10<sup>11</sup> m<sup>_−_3</sup> ) are present along the upper surface, and slightly lower number densities are present in the upper wake vortex. Comparably few particles are present in the lower wake vortex, where the diameter of average mass is maximized. Downstream of the wake vortices, gravitational settling eliminates all particles with diameter _>_ 35 _µ_ m. Unlike the microgravity case, the 8 cm _/_ s Earth gravity case is not bimodal, with a single local maximum at dp = 5 _µ_ m. Without particle settling, a bimodal distribution results with two local maximums at particle diameters comparable to the microgravity case. Rotating the heated vapor source 90<sup>_◦_</sup> dramatically reduces the wake volume, and no particles appear in the small wake. Rotating the heated vapor source does not affect the Earth gravity case as significantly as the microgravity case. Increasing the flow speed from decreases the prominence of buoyancy, delays interaction with the upper surface until further downstream, widens the particle size distribution to include particles on the nanometer scale, and introduces a second local maximum to the PSD near dp = 30 _µ_ m. 

Microgravity smoke presented in this study contains larger particles than terrestrial smoke. When the microgravity PSD is bimodal, the larger mode contains particles with dp _>_ 35 _µ_ m, while the terrestrial PSD contains effectively zero particles in this size range. Alternatively, the microgravity PSD can be unimodal with a mode diameter of dp = 10 _µ_ m – roughly twice that of the terrestrial case. Results exhibit three primary mechanisms that differentiate the microgravity and Earth gravity smoke: (1) different recirculation geometries and volumes modify the time-temperature-concentration history of the aerosol in regions of high temperature and high vapor mass fraction; (2) particle settling eliminates large particles from Earth gravity smoke, which reduces the maximum particle size and modifies aerosol development by changing coagulation and condensation rates of the remaining particles; (3) buoyant convection can transport condensible species toward relatively cool surfaces, increasing vapor-to-particle conversion. Each of these factors tend to increase the size of particles in microgravity relative to Earth gravity. However, each of these factors is also highly material- and geometry-dependent: (1) the wake dynamic becomes unimportant if nucleation occurs far downstream of the wake, or if there is a time dependent flow without a stable recirculation region; (2) particle settling is irrelevant if the condensing species forms particles that are too small to settle (the DBP considered in this work forms large particles); (3) if smoke is produced in an open environment, there may not be cool surfaces nearby to increase nucleation rates. In these situations, microgravity and Earth gravity smoke are hypothesized to be more similar. In

contrast, a quiescent, enclosed system is a relevant case where Earth gravity and microgravity smoke are hypothesized to differ significantly, and is a consideration of future work.
