###### Abstract: 

The investigation proposed here combines experiments and modeling to elucidate the fundamental mechanisms, phenomenology, and process conditions that govern the integrity and performance of solder joints produced in terrestrial vs. reduced gravity environments, such as the microgravity conditions onboard the International Space Station (ISS). The technical research program plans to utilize solder samples from the In-Space Soldering Investigation (ISSI) experiments from the Physical Sciences Informatics (PSI) repository [1, 2], as well as expand into other non-ISSI solder compositions, and combine space- and ground-based experiments with advanced 3D materials characterization, micromechanical testing, and mesoscale modeling. In particular, the project addresses the formation and persistence of porosity through the reflow/filling/freezing processes and the deleterious effects on microstructure and mechanical properties of the solder joint. It has been established [1, 2] that porosity arising from flux volatilization, which is dispersed and expelled from the solder joint under terrestrial gravity, may become entrapped within the freezing solder material under microgravity conditions, given the absence of buoyancy-driven convection. Our overall goals are (i) to advance the current qualitative understanding of this phenomenon into the realm of alloy/process-specific quantitative description and prediction and (ii) to examine the effects of mechanically and acoustically stimulated flow while assessing their potential effectiveness as porosity mitigation strategies for solder-based fabrication processes in space. Considering a range of potential applications and materials, 3 solder alloys will be investigated, including the ISSI lead-based (Pb-Sn) solders, as well as lead-free (Sn-Ag-Cu and Sn-Au) solders, which have recently shown promise for high-performance joint applications due to their good thermal, electrical conductivities and excellent corrosion, fatigue resistance [3-5]. 

Additionally, solder joints in the space environment are subjected to large temperature variations such as – 120 ºC to +25 ºC for the Mars missions, and – 157 ºC to +121 ºC outside ISS. However, as deep space exploration missions become longer and more distant, the solder and other metal joints' current reliability is inadequate for the harsh conditions they encounter. Therefore, this project focuses on following objectives to address the solder joints’ reliability in space: 1) Characterizing the effects of the lack of Earth's natural convective flow and buoyancy effects on solder-joint formation onboard ISS using the 40Pb-60Sn solders from In-Space Soldering Investigation (ISSI), 2) Examine the effects of mechanically and acoustically stimulated flow while assessing their potential effectiveness as porosity mitigation strategies for solder-based fabrication processes in space, and 3) Investigate the effects of elevated and cryo temperature conditions of space on solder properties. 

In this work, using two microgravity solders from the ISSI study, one ISSI terrestrial solder along with a third set of freshly made terrestrial 40wt%Pb-60wt%Sn solder, we report results from the microstructural characterization and resultant micro-to-nano mechanical response of solders in terrestrial vs. microgravity environments, 1g vs. ~1×10<sup>-5</sup> g under different temperature conditions. Results from the In-Space Soldering Investigation (ISSI) experiments performed aboard the International Space Station (ISS) have shown that soldering in microgravity is expected to be considerably different than their ground based counterparts due to Earth’s natural convective flow and buoyancy effects being minimized in microgravity during melting and solidification. On Earth, tests showed the solder melting sagging, dropping, or running down the test wire due to gravity effect (Fig 1a). During this process, the entrapped gases and water vapor evolving from the circuit board itself are transported to the joint surface and mostly eliminated while the joint is molten.

In comparison, aboard the ISS the molten solder was observed to form an equilibrium “football” shape (Fig. 1b). The changes in geometric shape are believed to be due to the dominance of surface tension in microgravity[2, 6, 7]. Shortly after equilibrium, the accrued flux was seen to spin around the sample’s surface. Liquid flux droplets rotating on sample surface gave indirect evidence of gravity independent thermocapillary flow, induced by internal temperature gradients within the molten solder ball. A flux bubble would migrate up a temperature gradient to the hottest spot, i.e. the components being joined. The trapped flux poses several concerns as it concentrates at sites like a repair joint, which can be detrimental to the desired strength as well as thermal and electrical conductivity from soldering. Also, the solder samples prepared aboard the ISS exhibited a considerable amount of internal porosity (Fig. 5) (about 13 times that of ISSI ground-based solders), as shown in the scanning electron microscope (SEM) micrographs in figure 6. The 3-D microstructural information from non-destructive micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) demonstrate the 3-D distribution of voids in the terrestrial solder (Fig. 4). The increased porosity is believed to be due to the lack of buoyant forces on flux and water vapor inherently present in the molten solder [1, 2, 6-8]. Entrapped gases will remain within the molten pool of solder until the solidification is complete, resulting in higher amount of voids, and compromising the mechanical integrity and electrical properties of the joint. Our current results also show a distinct difference of the distribution of the Sn-rich and Pb-rich, regions (Fig. 6) of the terrestrial vs. microgravity solders using electron-backscattered imaging. 

A further consideration, not mentioned in the earlier ISSI reports[1, 2, 6-8] is the substantial effect of aging on the solder microstructure and properties over the past 17 years. Previous investigations have shown that Pb-Sn soldering alloys (due to their low melting temperatures) exhibit grain coarsening and reduced mechanical properties with age, even under ambient conditions (room temperature). Microstructural changes stem from the diffusion of Sn in to the Pb rich regions, creating a finer dispersion of grains as well as voids near phase boundaries. After aging 30 days in ambient conditions the microstructure and mechanical properties appeared to stabilize [9]. In earlier NASA PSI reports, we have discussed the aging properties of 40Pb-60Sn Solder. In the report, we demonstrate how the microstructure and properties of solder joints change as a function of aging in 50%Pb-50%Sn (Fig. 13).

###### Project Motivation: 

Deployment of robust metal joining techniques in the space environment provide a solution towards sustainable, flexible missions through on-demand fabrication and repair of sustained damage to the spacecrafts, satellites, and habitat structures [10, 11]. However, long-duration space exploration missions in and beyond low earth orbit, such as Moon and Mars, present low gravity and an extreme temperature environment [12, 13] that requires novel metal joining solutions [1315] to deploy and expand human exploration. Therefore, it is necessary to consider the new inspace metal joining (such as in-space soldering, among other techniques) approaches to electrical and structural component repair that reduces the need for large replacement components. Such inspace manual component repair will be essential for future long-duration human exploration missions beyond low Earth orbit [16-19]. However, previous investigations have shown that metal joining in space differs considerably from in terrestrial conditions. 

Metal joining techniques in space are subjected to a) reduced gravity, b) reduced pressure, and c) a large temperature gradient than terrestrial metal joining. Additionally, the previous metal joining reports fall short of describing the fundamental structure-properties-processing relationships needed to develop robust in-space metal joining processes and process models. The persistent knowledge gap and the potential broad impacts associated with robust in-space metal joining capabilities clearly highlight the need for systematic investigation into the complex dynamics of melting, flow, volatilization, and solidification associated with metal joining in space. Therefore, this project's goal is to advance the current qualitative understanding of this phenomenon into the realm of solder alloy/process-specific quantitative description and prediction. 

In addition, metal joints in the space environment are subjected to large temperature variations such as   –120ºC to +25ºC for the Mars missions [20], – 230ºC to +120ºC for the missions to Moon, and – 157ºC to +121ºC outside ISS [21].  Similar to the human body system, the Mars exploration rovers cannot function well under extreme cold and hot temperatures. Therefore, to survive different phases of the mission, the rover's "vital organs," such as electronics/solder component, are situated inside a Warm Electronics Box (WEB) so that they do not get exposed to a temperature beyond ±40<sup>o</sup> C [20]. To save electric power for the other function of the rover as well as to use electronics of low cost, a distributed, non-heated architecture is being pursued to place the electronics/solder components close to the system they interact with during future missions. However, as deep space exploration missions become longer and more distant, the solder and other metal joints' current reliability is inadequate for the harsh conditions they encounter [21, 22]. 

As part of the ongoing endeavors to establish an electronics and mechanical joint repair capability for extended space missions, developing advanced techniques and materials for soldering components on circuit boards and small structures in microgravity environments is essential. Addressing the reliability of solder joints under these multi-extreme environments is critical for the success of future deep space exploration missions. Specifically, the solder joints used in onboard electronics must be able to withstand cryogenic temperatures and large variations in temperature. For example, ISS makes 16 orbits of earth in 24 hours, traveling through 16 sunsets and sunrises. Due to this, anything outside ISS experiences extreme cryo and elevated temperature 16 times in a day. Therefore, in this project, we build upon previous in-space metal joining reports [23-26] to understand the effects of the lack of Earth's convective flow and buoyancy effects during the melting and solidification onboard space station along with studying the effect of temperature on solder properties. Considering a range of potential applications and materials, 3 solder alloys will be investigated, including near eutectic lead-based (40Pb-60Sn), Off-eutectic (50Pb-50Sn),

###### Research Impact/Earth Benefits: 

The ongoing project is unique in that it combines both experimental and computational research on solder samples from the microgravity and ground based ISSI experiments, along with a comparative study on fresh solder samples. This project has the following research impact: (i) Our project objectives are a timely alignment with current NASA initiatives related to metals joining in reduced gravity environments. The three solder alloys for this work were chosen after extensive discussions with our NASA collaborators, and these alloy systems are currently being considered at NASA _as possible candidates for use in the NASA Mars Rover sample return missions_ . (ii) We also anticipate that the results from this project will inform experiment design responsive to the (future) NASA proposal calls such as the NASA Artemis program, which seeks to land the first woman and next man on the Moon. (iii) The insights gained from this work can be extended to study effects of fluid interfaces with surface tension variations that are also common for many spaceflight-enabling technologies (e.g., boiling, heat transfer, combustion, welding, brazing, or soldering). We envision that this project will contribute towards advanced understanding of multiple processes that typically rely on gravity-driven separations in the terrestrial environment, such as the removal of gas bubbles from a liquid, which must be accomplished by other means in microgravity environments. (iv) the developed gravity-level-specific protocols (GLSPs) for several configurations can be used by future scientists and flight personnel for studying metal joining techniques such as welding, brazing, or soldering under reduced gravity. 

###### Background: 

Metal joining is a process that uses heat to melt or heat metal just below the melting temperature to combine two or more pieces of metal together to create a larger and more complex structure. These metal joining methods include welding, brazing, soldering, and adhesive bonding. Welding involves melting the metal at the joint to be joined, which then cools and solidifies, fusing the two pieces together. Brazing is similar to welding, but instead of melting the metal at the joint, a filler metal is used to join the pieces together. Soldering is similar to brazing, but the filler metal has a lower melting point and is used for joining smaller and more delicate components. 

These metal joining processes such as in-space soldering are considered at NASA as possible techniques for the future long-duration missions such as _Moon's Artimis Program_ and _NASA's Mars Rover Sample Return Mission_ [27, 28]. NASA's Perseverance rover (currently seeking signs of ancient life in a large crater called Jezero on the Martian surface) is engaged in an audacious effort to bring samples of Martian rock and soil back to Earth. During the return voyage, the sample container will need to be packaged in a heavily protected capsule using metal joining processes – such as the in-space soldering processes (among other techniques) discussed in this proposal – in order to withstand re-entry into Earth's atmosphere. 

Deploying these metal joining techniques in the space environment will reduce the human crew's dependency on the ground support system, such that crew can perform electronics and structural component repairs during the mission without logistical support from Earth but with minimal mass and volume. However, previous investigations have shown that metal joining in microgravity gravity environments differs considerably than terrestrial conditions [1, 10, 29-31]. The physics of terrestrial metal joining has been studied widely, and physics-based predictions of resulting metal 

Page **7** of **35** 

joint structure and properties are robust [10, 13]. The heat and mass transfer in terrestrial metal joining is greatly driven by gravity-induced natural convection. Key physical processes in terrestrial conditions include a) Heat transfer from the heat source to the metal alloy, b) Evaporation of fluxes, c) alloy melting, d) natural, Marangoni, electromagnetic convection, e) solidification, and f) defect formation such as porosity, solidification shrinkage, hot cracking, segregation. These physical processes further affect the structure and properties of the metal joint. However, these physical processes are altered in microgravity relative to the terrestrial conditions due to a) reduced gravity, b) reduced pressure, and c) large temperature gradient than the terrestrial metal joining [10, 13]. Therefore, expanding the understanding of the metal joining in space is needed to understand the critical changes in physical processes and generate data for calibration and validation of computational models. 

<u>In-Space Soldering:</u> In soldering, an effective solder alloy will **a)** melt at a low temperature, readily wet the substrate components, and spontaneously flow into tight crevices under the action of capillary forces. In most commercial soldering processes, fluxes are necessary to suppress oxidation and to promote the desired wetting and spreading behavior, which is necessary for effective solder spreading and joint-gap filling during solder reflow. The **b)** non-destructive nature of the soldering process - with only shorttime exposure to moderate temperatures and no melting or dissolution of the components themselves – makes this process widely applicable to a wide range of fabrication and repair scenarios, both manual and automated. Decades of NASA’s mission success have been founded on the properties of Pb-Sn Figure 1: Solder preparation solders especially 40Pb-60Sn and 37Pb-63Sn. However, with aboard the ISS a) Wire wrap increasing legislature against lead due to its toxic effect on human solder b) Wire feed solder. health, The United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) also has lowered the Toxic Chemical Release reporting threshold for lead [32].  Due to rising concern over the use of lead in industry, Sn-Ag-Cu was invented [33, 34] by Dr. Anderson at the Ames Laboratory. It is a potential alternative to Pb-Sn solder for the Space applications due to good wettability, strength and thermal fatigue properties. 

Indeed, soldering is integral to many industrial processes, and joints produced with lead-based and lead-free [33] solder alloys are ubiquitous in electrical, electronic, structural, and flow/pipe applications. As a flexible and robust joining process that can be applied across numerous materials systems, soldering is a potential solution to many _in-space_ fabrication needs, including (i) in-flight repairs of electronic devices, plumbing systems, and structural components and (ii) sealing/resealing rigid containers with high structural/environmental integrity for long-term protective storage or transport. While the physical dynamics of soldering can be quite complex, an extensive and diverse experience base provides a sound framework for reliable process design in a typical terrestrial environment, under earth gravity with ambient temperature and pressure. The prospect of _in-space_ processing to produce consistent solder joints under µg conditions, however, presents numerous challenges. 

Unlike the broad ground-based experience with soldering processes, _space-based_ experience with in-service components is limited to a small number of solder repairs on the Mir Space Station and the ISS, and reports of related inspections or other service characteristics are not available [35]. 

Page **8** of **35** 

Likewise, reports of space-based experimental work is rather sparse, but some investigation has been done to assess the differences between the behavior of solders under microgravity conditions, as compared to those on Earth. Sounding rocket experiments, performed in the 1980s showed that capillary filling behavior for Pb-Sn solders was enhanced under reduced gravity, as compared to terrestrial conditions [36]. Subsequent Space Shuttle experiments, [37] further suggested that reduced buoyancy-driven convection may be responsible for entrapped flux in solder joints, an assertion supported by later parabolic flight experiments reported by Watson et al., where it was also shown that the type of flux and flux application procedure both influence the entrapment mechanism, where ground-based (low) porosity levels were most closely approached with a liquid flux that was heated and permitted to evaporate prior to solder wetting [35]. Experiments performed onboard the ISS [1, 2] revealed the dominant influence of thermocapillary flow in the absence of natural convection, further demonstrating the importance of joint configuration and process conditions in mitigation of the detrimental effects of solder joint porosity, such as the 32% decrease in joint shear strength associated with a 14% increase in internal porosity found by Thomas et al, comparing Sn-Ag-Cu joints produced in parabolic flights to those produced on the ground [38]. While a critical knowledge gap regarding the dynamics of pore formation and the influence of processing conditions persists, considerable efforts have been made toward mitigating the effects. For example, dispersion-strengthened or composite solders have been developed for enhanced strength, but these suffer from low ductility and do not offer the high fluidity and broad applicability to a range of joint configurations offered by monolithic alloy solders. Other strategies for reduction of porosity include induced flow by the action of a magnetic field on suspended magnetic (Fe or Fe-Co oxide) particles are currently being investigated [39]. 

The persistent knowledge gap and the potential broad impacts associated with robust in-space soldering capabilities clearly highlight the need for systematic investigation into the complex dynamics of melting, flow, volatilization, and solidification associated with soldering in space. The investigation proposed here aims to address this critical knowledge gap, advancing fundamental understanding and providing the quantitative relationships between solidification and flow characteristics, processing conditions, microstructure and porosity, and local/bulk mechanical properties for selected solder alloys. Therefore, this project builds upon the ISSI and utilizes samples from the ISSI experiments to enhance our understanding of soldering capabilities in microgravity. More details on ISSI experiments are given in the subsequent sections. In addition to applying novel materials characterization methods to _quantify_ the deleterious effects of porosity and related microstructure on joint performance, we will extend previously reported efforts to minimize joint defects through rational design of the soldering parameters such as the substrate/solder composition and thermal profile in our new ISS Flight Opportunity experiments in Fall/Winter 2023.

The experimental procedure consisted of 86 soldering experiments conducted on the ISS in 2003-04, and an equivalent set of ground-based experiments using identical equipment and supplies. The ISS experiments were performed by taking pieces of silver-coated copper wire and bending and twisting them into multiple test coupon configurations. The selected lengths of 40wt%Pb-60wt%Sn wire were cut and 1) wrapped around the test coupons and melted using the soldering iron 2) fed on the similar test coupons that were heated by the soldering iron (Fig.1). The ground experiments involved conductively melting 10 cm lengths of 40Pb-60Sn (3.3 wt% rosin flux) solder onto a loop turned into the silver coated copper wire. 

Results from the ISSI experiments performed aboard the ISS have shown that soldering in microgravity is expected to be considerably different than their groundbased counterparts due to Earth's natural convective flow and buoyancy effects being minimized in microgravity during melting and solidification [1, 40, 41]. On Earth, tests showed the solder melting, sagging, dropping, or running down the test wire due to the buoyancy (density) effect (Fig. 2a). In comparison, aboard the ISS, the molten solder was observed to form an equilibrium "football" shape (Fig. 2b). The changes in geometric shape are believed to be due to the dominance of surface tension in microgravity [2, 6, 7]. Shortly after equilibrium, the accrued flux was seen spinning around the sample's surface. As the solder started melting, the internal core of flux was released. When the solder was nearly melted, the released flux formed a drop and migrated to the bottom of the solder, which was closest to the heat source (soldering iron). Shortly after, the flux

droplet started to spin around the molten solder (Fig. 3a-c). The rotation rate increased steadily and then reached a plateau (Fig. 3). This spinning continued until the heat source was removed, after which the flux drop slowed down and eventually stopped. The variation in flux rotation in three samples (Fig. 3) was probably because of different temperature gradients imposed by different soldering iron tip positions on Cu wire. The heat is initially transmitted through highly conductive Cu wire, which melts the solder. The solder edge closest to the heat source is the hottest, while the opposite edge is likely the coldest, which causes the temperature gradient across the solder surface. Therefore, flux rotation on the sample surface gave indirect evidence of gravityindependent thermocapillary flow induced by temperature gradients across the molten solder ball in µg conditions. 

Also, under typical soldering conditions, rapid reflow temperatures results in flux volatilization and bubble formation. In the presence of gravity, liquid solders tend to mix vigorously during the reflow period due to natural convection. This is beneficial, as any voids or bubbles that form due to evaporated fluxes are quickly swept to free surfaces and are thus removed from the solder joint. However, the situation is worse in the reduced gravity environment of space ( ̴1×10<sup>-5</sup> g) where, due to minimized buoyancy, bubbles have less chance to escape, thus complicating any in-space fabrication and repair operations. The ISSI data has demonstrated that in the _absence_ of gravity, fluid motion is greatly reduced, driven mainly by solidification volume change, thermally induced density gradients, and Marangoni effects. Therefore, due to the lack of buoyancy force and slower

mixing in the absence of the natural convection, voids and bubbles are not swept away and become entrapped in the interior of the solder joint upon solidification in microgravity conditions. During the process of soldering, the liquid solder temperature near the substrate, which is in contact with the heater, is higher than that far away from the substrate/heater. Therefore, a distinct surface tension gradient will form with low surface tension in the high-temperature region and high surface tension in the low-temperature region. The effect of such a temperature gradient is predominant in the absence of the buoyancy force and natural convective flow [42]. Then the Marangoni force will tend to pull the voids towards the high-temperature regions such as the substrate.

In the absence of the buoyancy in the microgravity, the dominating surface tension and Marangoni force drive the void detachment as well as void motion which causes the entrapped voids in the microgravity solder. 

This entrapped porosity dramatically degrades the thermal and electrical properties of the solder and severely reduces the mechanical integrity of the joint. Such porosity is seen in conventional (Earth gravity) solder joints, but is much more prevalent in solder joints produced under reducedgravity conditions (Fig. 2) [23-26]. As a result, any solder joints made under reduced gravity (such as in-orbit repair of electronic devices, tubing, and mechanical joints) are at risk of having their electrical or mechanical performance characteristics substantially degraded by entrapped porosity, increasing the manifold associated risks of subsequent device failures on astronauts and equipment in the unforgiving environment of space. 

The ISSI experiments aboard the ISS present a unique opportunity to improve our understating of the effects of increased amount of porosity in the microgravity solder. Results from this study will enhance our fundamental understanding of the effects of surface tension driven convection phenomena during solidification processing operations such as brazing, soldering, and welding. Furthermore, the microgravity experiments represent a lowest gravity boundary condition. As such, these results could also be useful in predicting solidification behavior on other lower gravity environments (such as the moon or Mars).

The results of the microgravity soldering experiments can be considered as a benchmark for developing solidification theories [47-50]. The improved microstructural homogeneity [51] observed in the microgravity experiments could be used to refine existing theories about the solidification process and to develop new theories that better account for the effects of gravity. However, the effect of Marangoni convection has been overlooked as insignificant under terrestrial conditions due to the dominating natural convection. In the presence of voids, a gradient in the surface tension (σ) at the free surface of the void can induce the Marangoni convection, which can influence the microstructure formation, void mobility [24, 47], dendrite growth [52, 53], heat transfer [45, 54], solid-liquid interface morphology and segregation [55, 56], and particle accumulation [57, 58]. Since the variation of surface tension in solder with temperature is negative, the molten solder at higher temperature has a lower surface tension. Therefore, variation in surface tension at the free surface of the void induces a Marangoni force on the molten solder that causes the liquid flow from the high temperature (low σ) to the low temperature (high σ). 

The magnitude of the Marangoni flow due to the void depends on the bubble diameter [59]. Task D: Characterizing the effects of the extreme elevated and cryogenic temperature on solder-joint properties: 

Long-duration space exploration missions such as Mars rover missions have traditionally kept their electronics in a heated enclosure known as a "warm electronics box" (WEB) to protect them from the extreme cryo temperature on Mars [20]. However, future Mars missions are planning to use a distributed, non-heated architecture for the rover's electronics, where the components will be placed near the systems they interact with rather than in a heated enclosure [20]. The new approach relies on low-cost, commercially available, and space-qualified electronic packaging assemblies. However, these components must be capable of operating in a functional range of – 120ºC to +25ºC for the Mars missions, – 230ºC to +120ºC for the missions to Moon, and – 157ºC to +121ºC outside ISS. Therefore, this project will focus on studying the properties of 1) Pure Tin, 2) 40Pb-60Sn, 3) 50Pb-50Sn, and 4) 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu solder alloys at a) RT, b) Elevated temperatures, and c) Cryo temperatures. 

Investigate the effect of aging on microstructure and properties of solders under ambient conditions: 

<u>Ex-situ nanoindentation to understand the effect of room temperature aging on near eutectic 40Pb60Sn solder properties:</u> Due to the high homologous temperature (TRT/TM = 0.65) for Pb-Sn solder (TM = 183<sup>o</sup> C), classical elemental diffusion described by Fick's law initiates the grain coarsening [60] that can affect the performance and reliability of these solder joints for space applications. Therefore, after over 17 years in storage, the ISSI aged Pb-Sn solder samples are expected to exhibit grain coarsening, softening, and a thick IMC layer. Since aging happens in the solid state of the samples, aging behavior in microgravity solder is expected to be the same as terrestrial solder.  Therefore, to account for the effect of aging in 17year-old microgravity solder, we prepared fresh terrestrial solder samples using a similar method (Wire feed) as the ISSI experiments in order to study the aging properties of Pb-Sn solders at RT. 

To determine the effect of aging on 40Pb60Sn solders properties, two aging conditions were considered: 1) In **surface aging, the** Solder sample was cut, and the same cut surface was examined throughout the aging study, 2) In **bulk aging** , Intact solders were cut on the particular day of the aging and examined. Nanomechanical testing showed a continuous decrease in the solder Berkovich hardness (Fig. 9a) over the aging period, where hardness reduced from 0.19 GPa on day 1 to 0.15 GPa on day 483. # **<mark>Primary Objective:</mark>** <mark>Understand the effects of room temperature aging on the structure and properties of off-eutectic 50wt%Pb-50wt%Sn solder using scanning electron microscopy and nanoindentation.</mark> 

**Motivation: In-Space Electronics Repair and Metal Joining** 

- **for Space Missions** 

- Soldering is an essential metal joining technique for future extraterrestrial missions. _(NASA Mars Rover projects, manned missions to the moon and Mars.)_ 

- Can be used for fabrication and repair on missions. 

- Future experiments will be studying the effects of microgravity on off-eutectic solder. 

- _In-Space Soldering Investigation (ISSI)_ 

- Understand the effects of microgravity on microstructure and void formation on sample solidification by analyzing samples from the ISSI (’03 - ’04). 



- Previous work done in the group shows that _eutectic_ solder undergoes rapid aging at room temperatures.
