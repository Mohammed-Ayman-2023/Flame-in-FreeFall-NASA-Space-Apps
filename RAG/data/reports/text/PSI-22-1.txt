### **1. Overview** 

The Electric-Field Effects on Laminar Diffusion Flames (E-FIELD Flames) experiment is one of six independent experiments that make up the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project for which tests are conducted within the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR) on the International Space Station (ISS). 

The experiment’s Principal Investigator (PI) is Prof. Derek Dunn-Rankin of the University of California, Irvine (UC Irvine).  Dr. Yu-Chien (Alice) Chien, also of UC Irvine, served as the Project Scientist at the university.  Meanwhile, Dennis P. Stocker of the NASA Glenn Research Center is NASA’s Project Scientist (PS) for ACME including E-FIELD Flames and the rest of the ACME investigations.  In preparatory drop testing, etc., Dr. Fumiaki Takahashi of the Case Western Reserve University (CWRU) served as the Deputy Project Scientist.  For ACME’s spaceflight operations, the NASA science team includes Drs. Uday G. Hegde and Vedha Nayagam who are also with CWRU. 

The experiment had two Co-Investigators, the first of which was Prof. Felix Weinberg of London’s Imperial College.  Prof. Weinberg was a pioneer in researching the effects of electrical fields on flames in microgravity, but unfortunately died in 2012 (see http://www.imperial.ac.uk/news/117808/tribute-felix- <u>weinberg/) before the hardware was built and the tests were conducted on the ISS.  The second Co-</u> Investigator was Dr. Sunny Karnani who did his doctoral research in preparation of the spaceflight experiment and afterward worked for ClearSign Combustion Corp., which markets Electrodynamic Combustion Control (https://www.clearsign.com/technology/electrodynamic-combustion-control/).  The corporation provided some support to the E-FIELD Flames research effort, but Karnani left ClearSign to conduct research for the U.S. Army, effectively ending his intimate role in the project prior to the spaceflight operations.  He remains a collaborator. 

E-FIELD Flames operations were conducted in two rounds of testing during Increments 55-57, which were more specifically in March-May and September-November 2018.  The break in testing (from May to September) was intentional and planned years in advance to allow the research team to analyze early results and use the insights obtained from the analysis to plan the second round so as to maximize its scientific output. 

### **2. Science Overview** 

##### **A. Background** 

_With minor editing, this background is the Executive Summary from the experiment’s Science Requirements Document (SRD) of March 2010._ 

This project utilizes electric fields in microgravity combustion science to establish a fundamental understanding of chemi-ionization and ion-driven convection in flames. It also uses this understanding to demonstrate electrical manipulation of sooting behavior and stability limits in the absence of buoyancy. This work is based on prior foundation studies that included simulating zero-g combustion of small gas jet diffusion flames in an earth-gravity laboratory, and the electric field control of flame electrodynamics. The current SRD proposes to use the extended-duration microgravity environment to further develop an understanding of electric fields in combustion processes and their potential applications.

The research centers on the fact that electrical forces, acting on charge carriers created through chemi-ionization, can provide for measurement and manipulation of flames in microgravity environments. For example, using the drag exercised on flame ions, electric fields have demonstrated observable influences on flame shape, sooting behavior, burning velocity, extinction, and stability. The source of these influences almost certainly resides in ion wind effects. In microgravity, where there are no density-driven buoyant effects to contend with, ion winds represent an important (and perhaps the only) body force that can be applied to manipulate combustion systems. This project includes both academic study of electric field effects and aspects for practical utilization of electrical control of combustion processes. 

The prior laboratory experiments have shown that electrical properties of flames can be used to produce enhanced combustion performance by: (a) manipulating flame ions in order to change flame chemistry – this is generally considered a minor influence because flame ion concentration is typically small, but it may be critical at near-limit conditions; (b) creating a neutral wind (ion driven convection) locally at the flame front – this process can provide a local actuator for flame convection control; (c) using electric fields to affect soot formation and transport – ions and soot are closely linked because there are shared chemical pathways and because soot particles are charged by the flame ions; and (d) using electrical properties to characterize flames – e.g., there is a relationship between flame temperature, the carbon content of a fuel, and the ion current it can produce at saturation. 

In 1-g, the above-mentioned processes are confounded by complex buoyancy interactions that cannot be unraveled. Hence, to evaluate them in detail, we propose the following experimental objectives for an extended microgravity environment: (A) determine the relationship between electric field voltage and chemi-ion current as functions of total fuel flow rate and the amount of inert (nitrogen) in the fuel; (B) compare the time response of the flame’s chemiluminescence and ion current to rapid changes in electric fields; (C) determine sooting behavior in relation to the electric field influences; and (D) manipulate the limit behavior of lifted flames using electric fields. 

The experimental approach includes a simple gas jet diffusion flame and a co-flow burner. The basic variations in the tests are in the burner type, the fuel type (nominally nonsooting and sooting), and in the level of inert dilution of the fuel to vary the flame strength. The co-flow or environment gas will have a nominal molar composition of 21% O2 and 79% N2. The experimental concept is to ground the burner to the test chamber and to install an electrically active planar mesh electrode downstream of the burner mouth. The potential of the mesh electrode can be either positive or negative, with a steady voltage or with a time varying potential (including a rapid step change) to examine the flame’s steady state response and time response. The data to be recorded include the applied voltage, the ion current, the flame broadband luminosity, the CH* luminosity, flame images of OH* and visible light showing the flame shape as a function of electric potential and flame shape changes during transients in the electric field. In addition, a desired measurement of temperature in and near the flame will monitor the thermal field as a function of the electric field conditions. 

The data from these measurements will allow a chemical and physical understanding of flame ion production and information showing the potential to use these ions to electrically control the flame both via modification of the local chemistry near extinction limits and through changes in the local convection using the ion driven wind. For example, a lifted diffusion flame near its blowoff condition is sensitive to mixing at the flame anchor point. Therefore, electric fields acting

on near-limit flames can exert a large influence because they can affect the local mixing behavior. The role of electric fields on soot formation will be dramatic under conditions where the ion driven wind has a large influence on flow residence time (or on ion residence time, if chemical effects are found important) in sooting flames. 

Generally, the convective flows driven by electric fields acting on flame ions and those driven by buoyancy have been found to be of the same order of magnitude in earth gravity. In addition, raising the temperature often increases the ion production rate and the response to an electric field, but increasing temperature also increases the buoyancy effect, making it difficult, on earth, to separate ion wind effects from those due to buoyancy. In the zero-g environment, it will be possible to eliminate the buoyancy confusion and develop sufficient understanding to predict the response of flames to electric fields under all conditions. Extended run times are crucial for all of the studies because the timescales of flame property changes (which includes chemi-ionization) are dominated by convection (length scale/velocity) and past studies have shown that soot studies require high quality zero gravity timescales longer than is available in aircraft and drop towers. In addition, exploring instabilities and extinction behavior in near-limit flames requires experiments lasting tens of seconds. 

Overall, the goal of this zero-gravity study is to understand chemi-ionization behavior and the resulting ion driven winds sufficiently well so that electrical properties of flames can be used to characterize (by monitoring ion current) and control them (via direct chemical or local convective influences). Electric field effects on flames have been documented for decades (even centuries), but flames in 1-g all include buoyancy influences that affect ion generation behavior. For flames near transitions and limits, buoyant influences always confound any understanding of the role of ion driven winds in using electric fields to characterize and manipulate flames. The proposed study removes this confounding influence and allows definitive measurement and analysis of the electric field effects on jet diffusion and co-flow flames. 

##### **B. Rationale for Microgravity** 

_This is the ‘Justification for Extended-Duration Microgravity’ section of the experiment’s SRD from March 2010.  See the SRD itself for the references, etc. as they are not included in this report._ 

As described in the Background and Overview (Section 1.1), electric field effects and buoyancy effects have been found to be of the same order of magnitude. In addition, in some cases, raising the flame temperature will increase the O atom concentration, thereby increasing the ion production rate and increasing the response to an electric field. At the same time, increasing temperature increases the buoyancy effect, making it difficult to separate ion wind effects from buoyancy. In the zero-g environment, it will be possible to eliminate the buoyancy confusion and develop sufficient understanding to predict the response of flames to electric fields under all conditions. For electrically driven flames, microgravity is also necessary to create conditions that produce symmetric flames suitable for Abel inversion and steady-state analysis. While in general natural convection can enhance symmetry relative to non-buoyant flames, a flame at 1-g in an electric wind experiences buoyancy as a disruptive influence on symmetry. Even when buoyancy effects can be minimized, we have found that there are periodic disturbances that disrupt the ideal symmetry. Highly symmetric conditions of extended microgravity are also useful for numerically simulating these various effects by our electrokinetic model or more comprehensively by others.

Finally, in order to fully evaluate the potential for control, it is important to examine the effects separately. An example of the importance of microgravity for the electric field effects studies is shown in Figure 2.6. This figure compares the capillary flame used for 1-g experiments (which has some similarities to the jet-flame companion study) when it is oriented vertically to when it is oriented horizontally relative to gravity. These flames behave differently in shape, size, and ion production demonstrating the substantial influence played by buoyancy even in the relatively small capillary flames. Gravity effects in the co-flow flames have been described in the SRD for the CLD Flame experiment, and the same influences will be important when we apply electric fields. Only in extended microgravity will it be possible to separate the ion driven wind effects from buoyant wind effects.

Extended run times are crucial for all of the studies because the timescales involved extend beyond drop tower capabilities and the microgravity quality required for symmetry and stability exceeds aircraft microgravity. The electric-field flame interaction tests carried out in the NASA Glenn 2.2 Second Drop Tower tests by Yuan and Hegde [55] showed that the flame did not appear to reach steady state during the 2.2 sec. drop. Furthermore, their results showed substantial differences in flame shape between cases where the electric field was activated half-way through the drop and at the beginning of the drop, again suggesting that steady state was not achieved. Although, we varied the range of tests performed at the Drop Tower, our research group encountered similar obstacles. Drop times were insufficient to achieve a steady flame after voltage changes in one direction. Additionally, we were unable to capture the removal of soot from the ignition event and the subsequent response of the flame to the electric field. Furthermore, testing has shown path dependence in the response time of the flame when subjected to step voltages of varying polarities. Properly mapping the steady-state ion current as a function of applied voltage requires that the flame stabilize at each applied voltage. Generally speaking, the timescale of flame property changes (which includes chemi-ionization) is dominated by convection (length scale/velocity), which is on the order of 0.1 second. In order to ensure quasisteady conditions of the VI characteristics for a single flame configuration with some fidelity (e.g., 50 steps of 200 V each, with 2 seconds between steps for settling after the change), requires more than 100 seconds of continuous high quality microgravity. Each condition and voltage cannot be

run individually (i.e., with separate drop tests) because the 2 second settling time requires that the flame begin at a settled initial condition before taking another relatively small voltage step. If we tried to step to the initial condition, allow settling, take the base measurement, then step the voltage and again wait the required settling time, we would exceed 5 seconds for each test in nearly all cases. To run a return voltage sweep to examine hysteresis might take a further 100 seconds. Furthermore, these times are based on 1-g results which under low ion wind conditions are likely to be faster than occurs in microgravity since their the convective timescales can be substantially smaller (under low electric field forcing conditions, for example). 

Understanding the electric field effects on soot is another area that requires high quality zero gravity timescales longer than is available in drop towers. A variety of zero gravity diffusion flame experience has shown that quasi-steady soot formation requires many seconds [22, 56, 57]. The long duration is necessary since we are exploring the relative importance of residence time modification of these processes in these flames. In addition, as mentioned above, instabilities and extinction behavior in near-limit flames generally have a stochastic character so that relatively long observation times are needed to quantify electric field effects. For example, lifted flames in electric fields can exhibit an oscillatory behavior where they alternately approach and recede from the burner exit until they finally reattach. Tens of seconds for this process is not unusual. Extended microgravity conditions will clarify the role of electric fields (via ion winds) on stability limits of flames. 

##### **C. Science Objectives** 

_The proposed objectives text presented here is the ‘Experimental Objectives’ section of the experiment’s SRD from March 2010.  Although the objectives were not formally changed, the slow response time of the high-voltage power supplies (recommended in the ISRD) limited the ability to accomplish objective B.  To make up in part for this shortfall, the investigation was permitted to test with two different burner types (specifically gas-jet and coflow burners), where the use of multiple burner types was disallowed for ACME experiments to limit their scope.  Note that the hardware shortcoming was identified during the hardware development (and thus prior to spaceflight operations), but the project management decided to accept the shortcoming and proceed with the existing design to avoid the cost and schedule ramifications of a redesign._ 

The ultimate objective of this experiment is to develop an understanding of the mechanisms of chemi-ionization in flames and to describe the interplay between convective flows driven by those flame ions and their generation rate. With this information it will then be possible to detect and control flame behavior electrically. In 1-g, laminar diffusion flames are often controlled to a large extent by natural convection, and particularly by the rate at which natural convection brings fresh oxidizer to the reaction zone. Hence, the vigor with which combustion proceeds depends on local convection. As described in Section 1, an electric field acting on flame ions produces a body force and convection (analogous to buoyancy) which affects the combustion process. The change in the combustion process can in turn affect the ion production rate, which is responsible for the body force. This coupled interaction is very difficult to measure, study, and understand in 1-g because buoyant forces (of approximately equal magnitude) interfere. In microgravity, there is no such complication and the body force in the flame is associated entirely with the ion-driven wind. 1-g experiments have demonstrated clearly that electrically driven convection can be used to modify the local flow field in a manner that changes combustion behavior. For example, it can stabilize lifted flames or force them to reattach. Electric fields have been seen to change soot formation, 

7

though whether solely by residence time effects or their combination with chemical alterations has not been confirmed. In addition, ions can attach to soot particles providing them with enhanced mobility that can be manipulated. Furthermore, the electrical properties of flames provide a measure of their character. For example, non-premixed flames exhibit different relationships between ion production and ion wind forces than do premixed flames. The hydrocarbon fuel, and in particular, the carbon/hydrogen ratio, also affects these interactions. The goal of this project is to clarify and quantify this range of electrical effects and to then demonstrate the electrical manipulation of flame stability and soot in flames using the information obtained. 

To accomplish this scientific goal, there are four fundamental experimental objectives, in priority order, using a jet diffusion flame burner and a co-flow diffusion flame burner. The jet flame is the more critical configuration because it has a large 1-g data foundation and it can be more easily modeled as an ion and thermal source. In microgravity, however, it will be primarily driven by ion wind convection (no buoyant contribution and at its low fuel flow rates, the injection momentum dies rapidly with distance from the injector) which may limit its operating range. The co-flow flame allows a forced convection component to provide a wider range of flow conditions (fuel dilution and exit velocity) and a reproducible lifted flame for stability and soot behavior studies: 

**_A) Determine the relationship between electric field voltage and chemi-ion current as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and the amount of inert (nitrogen) in the fuel._** 

For the above determination, a low-sooting and highly sooting fuel are included, and the inert weakens the flame sufficiently to permit near-limit conditions at moderate flow velocities, where the effects of electric fields will be most acute. As shown in Figure 1.10, the resulting voltagecurrent (VI) curve (also known as the voltage current characteristic -  VCC) from these experiments will identify the voltage and current at which the ion current first saturates, the voltage at which the current begins to increase beyond saturation, indicating a change in combustion behavior, and the voltage at which the flame extinguishes. These data can be analyzed further to provide the ion production per carbon atom of fuel flux which describes the flame character (i.e., diffusion to partially premixed), and the ion wind strength for each condition. In addition to their direct interpretative value for calculating ion-driven winds and body forces, these data can also be used in electrokinetic simulations to compute ion mobility values consistent with measured ion currents and to help validate the numerical simulations of other researchers that include ion species and electrical effects in their flame calculations. 

###### **_B) Obtain the time response of the flame chemiluminescence, luminosity, and the ion current to large step changes in voltage, as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and amount of inert (nitrogen) added to the fuel stream._** 

These data provide the dynamic system behavior of the flame. This information comprises the time response of the flame (i.e., its surface area and luminosity) as compared to the time response of the ion current. These timescales can temporally separate the direct chemical effects of electric fields from ion diffusion and ion wind convective forcing. In addition, the system response is required to eventually build flame models suitable for active electrical control, assuming linear behavior over small ranges. 

**_C) Evaluate and manipulate sooting behavior (i.e., soot volume fraction and location of luminous soot) in relation to the electric field voltage and chemi-ion current as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and amount of inert (nitrogen) added to the fuel stream._**

The interaction between soot and electric fields is very complex because it combines the direct influence of electric fields on the chemistry, the ion wind effects on residence time and mixing, and the electric forces on the charged soot particles themselves. The zero-gravity environment, using the combination of the jet diffusion flame and the co-flow flame provides a wide range of conditions where these different influences can be allowed to dominate individually. For example, the forced convection environment of the co-flow burner will minimize the influence of the axial ion-driven wind, which helps to distinguish the direct electrical effects on ion chemistry in soot formation. In addition to demonstrating the direct control of soot behavior by electric fields, separating the influences can help distinguish between neutral molecule addition mechanisms of soot formation and those that presume the importance of ionic species. For example, changing the strength and polarity of the electric field can change the availability of ion precursors, and electric convection can manipulate reactants’ time/temperature history. In addition, charges will accumulate onto soot, and the electric field can then act directly on the soot particles to change their residence time and path through the flame. 

###### **_D) Use the variation of the chemi-ion current as a function of applied voltage to characterize the combustion behavior of near-limit lifted flames and use the associated ion-driven wind to stabilize or reattach these flames._** 

Because the chemi-ion current per unit flame area can be used as an indicator of flame strength, its measure (both average and fluctuating properties) with low electric field voltage can be used to detect flames approaching their limit condition. By adjusting the electric field, 1-g studies indicate that an ion driven wind can be used to reduce the liftoff height and stabilize the flame. This is an example of open loop electrical flame sensing, analysis, and control.

In this study, a high-voltage electric field is generated between the burner and a copper electrode mesh located above (i.e., downstream of) the burner outlet, as can be seen in Figure 4(b).  The circular mesh is energized at up to 10,000 volts with either polarity, where the burner serves as the electrical return and the flow of flame-produced ions completes the circuit.  The ion current between the burner and electrode mesh is measured from the voltage drop across a known resistor and application of Ohm’s Law.  With and without the copper ring, the electrode mesh is 8.9 and 8.4 cm in diameter, respectively.  The square copper weave has 7.1 wires/cm, a wire diameter of 0.43 mm, and a 48% open area.  The mesh permits gas to pass through while also acting as an electrical element.  The electrode mesh is mounted 37 mm above the coflow burner tip and is centered on and orthogonal to the chamber axis.  The ACME chamber insert can also

ACME’s non-imaging measurements are recorded at 125 Hz. The flame behavior is observed optically with a variety of instruments.  For most ACME experiments, including E-FIELD Flames, the radiant emission is measured with 6 thermopile detectors with a spectral range of nominally 0.2-11 microns.  Five radiometers are in a crewexchangeable linear array that is mounted parallel to the chamber axis.  The other detector is downstream and off axis from the burner.  Chemiluminescent emission is measured with a set of three Photomultiplier Tube (PMT) modules.  Their spectral range is 230-700 nm, i.e. in the visible and near ultraviolet, but two are equipped with narrow bandpass filters for measurement of OH* and CH* emissions, respectively.  The third PMT is equipped with a neutral density filter to prevent saturation, although the gain values for all three PMTs are independently and readily adjustable. Like many of the components, the array of PMT modules can be removed from the chamber insert by the crew.  In addition to their scientific value, PMT measurements are used operationally to detect ignition and automatically deactivate and retract the igniter.  The radiometer or PMT measurements can similarly be used to detect extinction and automatically end the gas flow and test. 

Imaging measurements of the flames complement the optical sensors described above.  Color imaging is provided by an analog color camera within the chamber and a high-definition (1360 x 1024), 12-bit color camera mounted outside the chamber on CIR’s optical bench.  The latter is equipped with a motorized lens with independent control of the zoom, iris, and focus.  It has a filter barrel with a fused silica window (for color imaging), a blue-green BG7 Schott glass filter, and bandpass filters for imaging CH* and HCHO (formaldehyde) chemiluminescence.  The digital color camera was characterized prior to launch for color-ratio pyrometry and measurement of the soot volume fraction, via the techniques of [19, 20].  The camera makes soot pyrometry possible as well as Thin Filament Pyrometry (TFP), where the chamber insert is equipped with a translating array of silicon-carbide (SiC) fibers.  A 14-micron fiber array can be seen at the top of Figure 4(b). However, the fibers interfere with the electric field preventing the use of Thin Filament Pyrometry in the E-FIELD Flames experiment. 

Two intensified monochrome cameras mounted outside the combustion chamber are available for ACME use, although only one is being used by E-FIELD Flames.  It is a high-definition (1390x1040), 12-bit camera equipped with a filter barrel similar to that used by the digital color camera discussed in the preceding paragraph, although it doesn’t have a blue-green filter.  The CH* filter has been used in the intensified photography of the E-FIELD Flames tests.  While unused by E-FIELD Flames, the CIR is also equipped with a high-definition (1024x1024) camera with an intensifier extending into the ultraviolet which can be equipped with a bandpass filter and corresponding optics for OH* imaging. 

Finally, a high-definition (1024x1024), 12-bit monochrome camera is mounted outside the chamber opposite a collimated light source with two fiber-coupled laser diodes emitting red light

##### **C. Hardware Shortcomings** 

As previously discussed, the slow response time of the high-voltage power supplies (recommended in the ISRD) did not meet the requirements necessary to achieve the requested time response performance, and as such inhibited determination of the flame’s response time to rapid changes in the electric field strength. 

The negative and positive high-voltage power supplies were to have been coupled, as described for example in Appendix C of the ISRD.  However, switching between the two high-voltage power supplies led to excessive Electromagnetic Interference (EMI) so the power supplies were completely decoupled, where only one was installed at a time.  As such, testing was limited to a single polarity at a time. 

The imaging for soot volume fraction via light extinction was largely blocked by the bracket holding the electrode mesh, where this was a design trade-off necessitated primarily by ACME’s large number of cameras and other optical instruments.  However, Prof. Marshall Long (Yale

University), who is also an ACME investigator, has an alternate technique for determining the soot volume fraction from ACME’s data camera (DataCam) imaging. 

The DataCam is unfortunately not as sensitive as had been hoped to the dim microgravity flames. Therefore, the imaging with that camera has typically been at a long exposure time, e.g., 0.65 s, even though it is a video camera capable of 30 frames per second (fps).   The principal exception has been when making soot pyrometry or Thin Filament Pyrometry (TFP) measurements. 

Between its thickness and the anti-reflection coating, the DataCam’s blue-green filter excessively attenuates the red light making its use undesirable.  The filter’s purpose intended was to balance the intensity of the three colors in flame imaging to facilitate pyrometry.  The accuracy of the pyrometric temperature measurements was diminished as result of this shortcoming.

##### **A. Changed Test Plans** 

To limit their scope, ACME investigations were limited to a single burner type and the E-FIELD Flames tests identified as required in the ISRD were with a gas-jet burner.  However, during the hardware development (and thus prior to spaceflight operations) it was determined that the slow response of the high-voltage power supplies (suggested in the ISRD) inhibited determination of the response time of the flame to the electric field, i.e., objective B.  To make up for that science loss, the experiment was permitted to conduct a portion of its tests with ACME’s coflow burner, where such tests had been identified as desired in the ISRD.  The gas-jet burner used for the E- FIELD Flames investigation matched the inner tube of the coflow burner.  Therefore, the tests conducted allowed assessment of both the coflow and jet entrainment, where tests with the coflow burner were carried out with and without a coflow. 

It had also been conceived that some tests would be conducted in which the polarity of the electric field was varied.  However, the switching between the two high-voltage power supplies led to excessive Electromagnetic Interference (EMI).  Therefore, the electrical design was altered such that the power supplies were completely decoupled, where only one was installed at a time. This avoided the excessive EMI but prevented changes of polarity within a test, as had been planned for the step response procedure described in Table 4.2.2 of the ISRD.  It also led to the need for crew to swap the power supplies to enable testing at each polarity. 

To be clear, these significant changes to the experiment were made prior to the spaceflight operations where the decisions were made by the project management to manage the budget and schedule. 

In addition to the above hardware-driven changes to the test matrix, a series of other smaller changes ensued as choices for more consistent scientific outcomes were sought.  All adjustments were based on finding more effective ways to utilize the space time experiments.  The change history included the following.

##### **B. Overview of Experiment Test Operations** 

_The narrative description of the operations is from the ‘Methods / Experimental’ section of the following conference paper:_ 

_Y.-C. Chien, J. Tinajero, D. Stocker, U. Hegde, and D. Dunn-Rankin, “Electric Field Effects on Flames in Microgravity on the International Space Station,” 2018 Spring Technical Meeting of the Central States Section of The Combustion Institute, Minneapolis, MN, 20-22 May 2018._ 

_See the paper itself for the references, etc. as they are not included in this report.  As a minor note, the size of some figures was adjusted in this report, but none were cropped._ 

The CIR experiments are configured, but not conducted, by the ISS crew.  Thus far, ACME’s activity has included the majority of the crew members, regardless of their nationalities.  Most often the astronauts and cosmonauts exchange gas bottles, mass flow controllers, and igniter tips.  In the latter case, the igniter tip exchange can occur after failure or proactively before failure when the crew already has changes to make changes in the interior of the insert.  While these activities may seem mundane, the crew play a critical role in setting up the hardware for each experiment.  As an extreme example, there were crew activities on 8 separate days in Sept.-Oct. 2017 to configure the CIR and ACME hardware for the first ACME experiment, namely the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment.  In contrast, only two crew days were required to reconfigure the hardware for the first E-FIELD Flames tests because of the initial use of the same coflow burner. Planning is critical because crew activity is scheduled three weeks in advance, for which detailed procedures must be prepared and uplinked to the ISS. 

All CIR tests are remotely commanded from the ground from NASA’s Glenn ISS Payload Operations Center (GIPOC) in Cleveland, Ohio.  Typically, four to six people staff four consoles during the testing.  The CIR hardware is controlled from the Rack Officer (RO) and Data Management Officer (DMO) consoles, and the ACME hardware is controlled from the Payload Developer (PD) console. Two shifts of personnel are scheduled to staff these three consoles on test days because they typically last 14 hours with the many pre- and post-testing activities.  Given that the tests are remotely commanded, the operations are carried out in the day and evening in Cleveland, e.g., from 7 AM to 9 PM.  A day’s tests, i.e., with flames, are normally conducted in a period of 10 hours of less.  Tests are normally conducted on three weekdays per two weeks.  On intervening weekdays, the image data is downlinked and the crew often swap one or both gas bottles. Transferring and downlinking data from a day’s tests also requires a 2-shift day for the RO and DMO consoles, where it is always necessary to verify that data was downlinked without errors and that data is not missing.  Those two positions are also staffed to guide and oversee the crew activity.  As a result, the CIR consoles are normally actively staffed on four weekdays per week.  A team of four carry out the detailed planning for the CIR and ACME console staff, and the daily operational plans typically exceed 50 pages on days including testing. 

The science console allows for review of the analog video and non-imaging telemetry (at 1 Hz) in near real time but doesn’t allow for commanding.  In the case of E-FIELD Flames, arrangements were made so that the analog video is streamed to a secure computer at UC Irvine to allow the research team to participate in test operations and to recommend adjustments to the day’s planned parameters.  That team does not see the telemetry during the testing, but is in communication via phone and e-mail with their NASA research partners at the GRC ISS Payload Operations Center (GIPOC).  While there are many preparatory steps, the tests are ultimately conducted using an uploaded software script and parameter files.  In advance of the test day, the

It is also possible to effectively conduct tests in a ‘manual’ mode, where parameters are changed in response to the analog video downlink or telemetry.  For example, the electric field potential can be adjusted to approach a stability or soot limit. 

Typically, ten E-FIELD Flames tests are conducted per test day. Despite the short test durations, the low rate results from several unavoidable factors. This paper reports on early results from the first round of E-FIELD Flames. 

While nitrogen is available from the space station, the combustion testing is limited by the available gas bottles that were and are launched to support the tests.  For E-FIELD Flames and most of ACME’s experiments, the test matrices used to plan those gas resources were established in 2010. As such, the general target was 50 tests per experiment.  For most ACME experiments, the limiting gas constraint is the oxygen/nitrogen bottles used to fill the chamber.  This resource constraint is exacerbated by the CIR because it is equipped with a pair of redundant safety timers to limit the fuel that can flow into the combustion chamber (where the timers can be set for the oxidizer flow in the case of inverse flame testing).  The timers can only be reset by venting the combustion chamber. For E-FIELD Flames, the safety timers are set to the maximum value of 999 seconds, which corresponds to the 10 tests typically completed per day.

As one of the projects of Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME), the E-FIELD Flames experiments were initiated in March and completed in November of 2018 with a series of flame studies on the coflow burner and the jet burner within the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR). The coflow burner, presented in this paper, consists of a small central fuel jet and a concentric outer ring flowing with synthetic air. ###### **Field direction and polarities** 

The electric field is established between the two electrodes, burner and mesh, which are connected separately with positive and negative polarity, as the test requires.  Because this research has no gravitationally relevant direction, any orientation can serve as the spatial reference coordinate.  The vast majority of research in electric field related combustion study is conducted at earth gravity, where the buoyancy is one of the key properties considered and repeatedly discussed.  In microgravity, a facing upright burner is equivalent to one facing upside down (or even sideways) scientifically because the buoyancy/gravitation force is zero.  To maintain the concept where the nominal field strength coincides with the direction of an ion driven wind that results from positive ions flowing from high to low potential, the positive field direction aligns with the fluid flow direction (where the jet flows) from burner to mesh, as positive ions repel from the burner (Enominal= -V/d); a negative electric field is the opposite direction from mesh to burner, and the positive ions move toward the burner, as indicated in Figure 7.

The first set of results show the voltage-current (V-I) curve for flames in microgravity.  The curves are qualitatively similar, with a parabolic increase in ion current with increasing nominal field strength, up to a peak.  Following the peak, increasing the voltage creates a distinct decrease in ion current, towards a saturation plateau, where the ion current remains constant with increasing field strength.  Although the plateau is not apparent in Figure 8, it is clearly seen in all cases when a sufficient field strength is applied (e.g., Fig. 9), where the sufficient field strength depends on the fuel flow rate and coflow.  In all the curves, the jagged features are spikes following a voltage step change that are mostly associated with the transient capacitance of the system when rapid steps are requested.  These overshoots notwithstanding, the V-I curves follow smooth and reproducible trajectories.

1. The ion current peaks at approximately the same field strength for both velocities in 1-g while in microgravity the ion current peak for a lower fuel velocity occurs at a lower field strength, where the field strength at the peak current seems to scale linearly with the fuel velocity.

##### **D. Surprises and New Discoveries** 

We expect many more discoveries to arise as we sort through the detailed data sets, but the initial surprises and discoveries include: 

(1) The behavior of the V-I curve is distinctly different in microgravity as compared to 1-g flames, where in microgravity there is not a distinctive saturation ion current plateau but rather the flames exhibit a distinct peak ion current that corresponds to the most compact flame.  These results show that buoyancy driven flows are an important influence on 1-g flames even when an electric field is applied. 

(2) When soot is present in a flame, electric fields create a wide range of unpredictable behaviors that include elimination of the soot through what appears to be enhanced mixing for combustion, showers of luminous particles that are likely to be incandescent soot agglomerates, and unstable dancing flames.  Sooting is far more significant in flames in microgravity than is seen in similar conditions with 1-g flames. 

(3) There are clear correlations between flame luminosity, combustion intensity, and ion current. The results suggest that the most robust burning also produces the highest ion current, even though the flame is most compact. 

(4) The very weak low fuel concentration flames were stabilized at ignition with very small electric fields, where without the fields the flames would quickly quench.  This stabilizing feature of electric fields for weak zero gravity flames was a surprise, and it suggests that electric fields may be a good tool for approximating buoyancy body forces at partial gravity. 

(5) Jet flames under the influence of the electric field were unstable at high field strengths, and these flames experienced corona and then arc discharges due to the large field concentration near the jet burner tip.  The flame behavior was dramatic but difficult to characterize, suggesting a spatially- and time-varying field/flame interaction.

##### **E. Accomplishment of Approved Objectives** 

All major science objectives were accomplished.  As identified below, objectives A, B, C, and D, were completed with differing degrees of success. 

###### **_A) Determine the relationship between electric field voltage and chemi-ion current as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and the amount of inert (nitrogen) in the fuel._** 

This was accomplished through the collection of V-I curves at all experiment conditions.  This objective was completed with great success. 

###### **_B) Obtain the time response of the flame chemiluminescence, luminosity, and the ion current to large step changes in voltage, as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and amount of inert (nitrogen) added to the fuel stream._** 

Although the power supplies were not capable of rapid downward voltage changes, the upward steps were sufficiently fast to permit the time response evaluation.  In microgravity, the preliminary results suggest that the time response is faster than is seen in 1-g suggesting that the changes in the buoyancy driven flows play a role in the time response of these flames on earth. The scientific understanding associated with this objective was sufficient. 

###### **_C) Evaluate and manipulate sooting behavior (i.e., soot volume fraction and location of luminous soot) in relation to the electric field voltage and chemi-ion current as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and amount of inert (nitrogen) added to the fuel stream._** 

This objective was only partially completed because the key soot volume fraction instrument was unavailable to the experiment because of the obscuration of the High-Bitdepth Multispectral (HiBMs) camera by the bracket holding the electrode mesh.  The location of the luminous soot was identifiable, however, so the qualitative results were completed.  There is, however, the potential to use additional calibration of the ACME color camera and the associated pyrometry and total luminosity to provide the temperature and soot volume fraction from the images that were collected.  This objective was met sufficiently. 

###### **_D) Use the variation of the chemi-ion current as a function of applied voltage to characterize the combustion behavior of near-limit lifted flames and use the associated ion-driven wind to stabilize or reattach these flames._** 

Both aspects of flames mentioned in the objective were determined.  As mentioned above, the electric field was definitely successful in stabilizing flames that would otherwise quench after ignition or not burn stably.  There was not an opportunity to produce true near-limit lifted flames, but the effect of the ion-driven wind for generating more intense combustion and compact flames was clearly demonstrated.  This objective was met sufficiently. 

The formal success criteria were presented in Section 2.D.  As can be seen from the science objectives completed, the project definitely met the high success criteria and partially met the complete success criteria.  Complete success was only missed because, as described under the objectives, only some time response data was obtained, and there were specific cases where the ion-driven wind helped stabilize flames that would otherwise be difficult to achieve a sustained ignition.  The electric field was also able to blow out flames under some conditions.  The response time of the power supplies, and the inability to switch polarity during a single experiment

##### **F. Additional Objectives Achieved** 

As we became more familiar with the conditions that provided the most impactful data, we reconstructed some test cases to provide more comprehensive matching of fundamental conditions.  For example, it is known that flames of similar character (i.e., temperature and intensity) have saturation ion currents that scale with the input carbon to the flame.  This is because the dominant chemical pathway to the key ion species includes HCO+.  Hence, to examine this feature of flames under electric field influence, we ran additional conditions with matching input carbon content. 

**Matching carbon content with higher or lower flow rates:** In addition to the core objectives achieved, experiments were run to match the carbon content of flames as a test for the role of fuel carbon in the ion-current peak production.  Classical theory shows that ion current scales with carbon content for premixed flames, and some 1-g data shows similar trends for diffusion flames, but no one had determined if the concept held for zero-g flames. 

**Flame response hysteresis:** We also obtained data for flames where the electric field was ramped up and down to determine any effects of hysteresis in the ion current or flame response.  This was an additional objective achieved. 

##### **G. Impacts, Benefits, and Products** 

The impact of this work is, as the original objectives identified, the delivery of a unique data set of chemi-ion behavior in flames under sufficiently stable and symmetric conditions that models and theories can be evaluated against the results.  As an example, the very distinctive shape of the V-I curve in microgravity, when matched in models, will give a very good indication that the feedback between ion driven wind and changes in flame shape and burning behavior that then further change the ion current is captured accurately. 

These results have already garnered the interest of other groups working on electric field effects on flames. 

The use of electric field effects on flames can be as sensor (variations of ion current as associated with combustion behavior) or actuator (ion-driven wind changes the flame shape and characteristics).  This work, as was originally planned, provides quantitative evaluation of the sensing and response potential of electric fields in combustion.  The applications can include the control of flames.

### **Follow-On Experiments** 

We hope to have a third round of testing so that we can concentrate on flames in the coflow burner configuration that we found to be the more useful and symmetric than those with the jet burner.  It is likely that the more uniform electric field is responsible for this observation. 

As noted above, useful results regarding the flame response time were obtained despite the slow response time of the power supply time through tests with fast upward voltage steps in some configurations.  The step response is not as comprehensive as we would like, however, to confirm the links between the ion driven wind as actuator and sensor. 

For objective D, we obtained very interesting information about flame stabilization and destabilization using electric fields but not in the manner we expected.  Rather the destabilizing influence of the field producing fluctuations in the flame was more pronounced a phenomenon than we had seen when buoyancy was also part of the body force involved.  We did not get to limit flames except that we showed clearly how in microgravity a flame can be stabilized with a small field rather than simply expanding and extinguishing.  So that is some information again at the limit.  We can discover more by concentrating on very low field strengths. 

The outcomes of the first two rounds justify further testing because the results were so successful. The success showed how perfectly the E-FIELD Flames experiment fits the capabilities and limitations of the CIR.  It is a configuration that can use the imaging capabilities at their current performance effectively.  The experiments manipulate flames between quasi-steady behaviors in microgravity rather than passively observing non-steady flames evolving.  The first rounds of testing spanned the experimental configuration space in terms of gases, flow rates, field ranges, and burner types.  These spanning experiments mapped the phenomena but did not allow a deep dive into the conditions that showed the most dramatic changes of flame character with changes in electric field strength.  In addition, excessive amounts of soot made unraveling some of the gas phase ion chemistry more complicated in the pure fuel cases, so we can now concentrate on moderate sooting cases.  In general, the bonus tests let us go back to the known "best case" situations and resolve the processes (e.g., flame shape, luminosity, soot formation and disappearance, ion current).  That is the matrix we proposed. 

It is not within the scope of the current work, but we continue to advocate for a Schlieren imaging capability in zero-g combustion systems.  The ability to image hot gas flows would benefit a range of experiments and would be particularly useful for the flames in electric field situations because alternative methods for determining flows would not be possible in the high field environments. It may be that the 5-second drop facility is appropriate for many of these companion tests.

#### **I. Introduction** 

The Electric-Field Effects on Laminar Diffusion Flames (E-FIELD Flames) experiment is conducted in the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR) on the International Space Station, as part of the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project.  In this experiment, an electric field with voltages as high as 10,000 volts is established between the burner and a flat, circular mesh electrode suspended above (i.e., downstream of) the burner.  The motion of the charged ions, which are naturally produced within the flame, are strongly affected by a strong electric field.  The resulting ion-driven wind can dramatically influence the stability and sooting behavior of the flame.  Measurements are made of electric-field strength, the ion current passing through the flame, and flame characteristics such as the size, structure, temperature, soot, and stability.  Conducting the tests in microgravity allows for great simplifications in the analysis of ion-driven convection and flame interaction, enabling new understanding and the development of less polluting and more efficient combustion technology for use on Earth.

An electric field can strongly influence flames because of its effect on the ions produced as a result of the combustion reactions. The induced ion wind can modify the flame shape, alter the soot and stability limits, and direct the heat transfer.  The purpose of the E-FIELD Flames experiment is to gain an improved understanding of flame ion production and learn how the electric field can be used to manipulate the ions and control non-premixed flames for practical benefit.  For example, electric fields can enable stable burner operation at fuel-lean conditions with reduced pollutant emission, i.e., where the flame is not reliably sustained without the electric field.  As another example, the role of electric fields on soot formation can be dramatic. It appears that the ion driven wind alters the local convection and strongly influences the residence time of the soot precursors within the hot flame.  The experiment contributes to our critical understanding of combustion processes in the presence of electric fields. 

More specifically, E-FIELD Flames has the following experimental objectives: 

- A. Determine the relationship between electric field voltage and chemi-ion current as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and the amount of inert (nitrogen) in the fuel. 

- B. Obtain the time response of the flame chemiluminescence, luminosity, and the ion current to large step changes in voltage, as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and amount of inert (nitrogen) added to the fuel stream. 

- C. Evaluate and manipulate sooting behavior (i.e., soot volume fraction and location of luminous soot) in relation to the electric field voltage and chemi-ion current as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and amount of inert (nitrogen) added to the fuel stream. 

- D. Use the variation of the chemi-ion current as a function of applied voltage to characterize the combustion behavior of near-limit lifted flames and use the associated ion-driven wind to stabilize or reattach these flames. 

The effect of the electric field on the flame is studied through tests conducted with a range of flame and electric field conditions.  The fuel, fuel jet velocity, air flow, electric-field voltage and polarity are the primary parameters of the E-FIELD Flames experiment.  Tests are conducted with methane and ethylene, including nitrogen dilutions of each, which provide a broad range of sootproducing conditions, i.e., from soot-free to highly sooting.  They are conducted with both a simple gas-jet burner and the coflow burner that was also used for the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment.  Tests with the coflow burner is conducted with and without a coflow of air, where the results of the latter are compared with those from the gas-jet burner to assess the effect of air entrainment on the flame.  The coflow burner also provides a different electrode configuration (close to a plane-to-plane) than the jet burner (close to a point-to-plane). In a typical test, the mesh electrode is energized at increasing potentials (relative to the burner), where the flame’s response (e.g., ion current, luminosity, shape) to the resulting field strength is measured.  The field strength is changed in repeated steps or is instead smoothly ramped. 

Microgravity studies of flames allow for a range of simplifications and provide various advantages over normal-gravity studies.  For example, the flicker of flames, which is a buoyancy-driven (i.e.,

gravity-driven) instability, is eliminated yielding quasi-steady flames in microgravity. Microgravity flame flicker is then the result of electric body forces alone.  Length scales are increased in microgravity flames facilitating analysis of the flame structure.  Momentumdominated flames, which are important for most practical combustion, can be studied at low velocities to simplify analysis.  Microgravity flames tend to have a much stronger sensitivity to their atmosphere and exhibit a much broader range of characteristics than normal-gravity flames because of the near absence of buoyant entrainment.  The long residence times in microgravity flames can lead to strong soot production, but many microgravity flames are soot free.  The absence of buoyancy makes microgravity flames advantageous for studies of limit and stability behavior where chemical kinetics are important.  With these advantages, microgravity testing can lead to new understanding of the complex nature of combustion – regardless of the gravitational environment of the combustion application.  Chemi-ion species, electronically excited chemiluminescent species are very low concentration in typical flames, making the reduced buoyancy environment valuable for evaluating the minor species chemistry. 

#### **II. Methods/Research Operations** 

##### **A. Discussion of Method/Protocol** 

E-FIELD Flames and other ACME experiments are configured, but not conducted, by the ISS crew.  The crew’s role is to set up the hardware, gas bottles, etc. for each set of tests. The most common changes include the gas bottles on the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR) and the mass flow controllers on the modular ACME chamber insert. 

All testing is commanded from the ground, specifically from the Glenn ISS Payload Operations Center (GIPOC).  The science portion of the testing is typically conducted in a nominally automated mode using pre-programmed scripts.  However, it may alternately be conducted in a ‘manual’ mode, where changes are made to a control parameter in response to the analog video downlink and the numerical data available in the telemetry stream.  For example, the mesh voltage can be adjusted to approach a flame’s stability or soot limit.  This was not done during Increments 55-56, where the console commanding during a test was typically limited to halting the test when it was observed in the analog ops video that the flame had extinguished. 

Testing is not conducted when thrusters fire or spacecraft dock or undock from ISS to ensure an acceptable acceleration environment. 

Data is downlinked between each set of tests, i.e., day of testing, to allow for preliminary analysis and requisite planning for subsequent testing.

#### **III. Results** 

##### **A. Baselined Activities Completed – Pre-flight, In-flight, Post-Flight** 

A total of 142 flames were ignited for the E-FIELD Flames experiment during Increments 55 and 56.  The first round of testing occurred from March to May 2018, which was followed by a second round, which began in September 2018 and was still underway at the beginning of Increment 57.  The first round of tests resulted in 120 ignited flames conducted with the same coflow burner used by the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment, where CLD Flame’s second round occurred between the two rounds of the E-FIELD Flames experiment.  The ‘interruption’ in the E-FIELD Flames testing was intentionally planned years in advance to enable the PI team to review the initial results in detail and with that knowledge plan the second round of tests to maximize its scientific outcome. 

E-FIELD Flames does not have living subjects like a life science experiment, but the tests are independently conducted with two different gaseous fuels, methane and ethylene. Both are simple hydrocarbon gases for which the chemistry is well understood.  Methane (CH4) is the primary component of natural gas and tends to produce low levels of soot given the absence of carbon-carbon bonds.  Meanwhile, ethylene (C2H4), is highly sooting because of its carbon double bond.  For both fuels, tests were conducted with pure fuel and fuel mixed with nitrogen over a broad range of dilutions.  For limited cases, the fuel and nitrogen was premixed on the ground prior to launch.  ACME bottles were launched with methane concentrations of 30%, 40%, 45%, and 100% with a balance of nitrogen (on a volume basis).  Similarly, bottles were launched with ethylene concentrations of 30%, 50%, and 100%, again with a balance of nitrogen (on a volume basis).  But the ACME hardware also allows for simultaneous flow of fuel and nitrogen to the burner, allowing for on-orbit fuel dilution and testing at a continuum of fuel concentrations.  In all cases, the coflow was 21% oxygen with a balance of nitrogen (on a volume basis). 

There are no samples (e.g., of soot) collected for E-FIELD Flames, where all of the ISS results are electronically transmitted to the ground.  That is furthermore the case for all currently planned ACME experiments.

##### **B. List of Preflight, In-flight, Postflight Anomalies** 

###### **1. Mission anomalies** 

The primary hard drive for the CIR’s IPSU-G Image Processing and Storage Unit - for Gigabit Ethernet (GigE) cameras – failed to boot on April 27, 2018 (18117).  For ACME experiments, the IPSU-G is used for two key cameras, the ACME color digital ‘data’ camera (i.e., DataCam) and the Cool Flames Investigation (CFI) intensified camera, which is used for CLD Flame with a narrow bandpass filter to image CH* chemiluminesence.  Use of the IPSU-G was recovered by astronaut Scott Tingle on May 7 (18127) by the removal of that primary drive.  The removed drive was reinstalled on Aug. 14 (18226) by astronaut Ricky Arnold during the second round of CLD Flame testing.  Although the hard drive was installed in the secondary position, the IPSU-G surprisingly again became unusable.  Therefore, it was removed on Aug. 22 (18234) by astronaut Alex Gerst, returning it to use, albeit without the intended storage capacity.

The CIR’s IPSU-1 Image Processing and Storage Unit, which is used with the High BitDepth Multispectral (HiBMs) camera and the collimated illumination package, was only intermittently operable in mid-Sept. 2018 and was not operable at the start of E- FIELD Flames’ second round.  Those capabilities were not seen as critical for E-FIELD Flames, where the illumination is significantly blocked by the mounting bracket for the mesh electrode.  Therefore, the tests on Sept. 26 (18269) and Oct. 1 (18274) were conducted without HiBMs imaging or the CIR illumination. ###### **2. Any other difficulties in obtaining scientific data** 

E-FIELD Flames tests sometimes automatically abort (due to the controlling software script), where that is typically prior to gas flow and the activation of the igniter.  As a result, there is little lost other than the time in the failed test attempt.   The aborts normally occur because of communication issues with IPSU-1.  These are infrequent, but there are some days when tests abort repeatedly. 

Tests are of course also unsuccessful when the flame fails to ignite.  The first test of the day has sometimes failed to ignite, presumably because the fuel line has not been sufficiently primed.  Ignition is sometimes unsuccessful because the settings for the automatic flame detection are such that detection threshold is reached because of the igniter glow rather than a flame.  As a result of the detection, the hot-wire igniter is deactivated and retracted, which is of course an issue if the flame has yet to ignite. This is typically avoided by detecting ignition with measurement of the flame’s OH* chemiluminescence with a Photomultiplier Tube (PMT) module.  The OH* chemiluminescence, in the ultraviolet at 310 nm, is a good indicator of an ignited flame rather than the glowing igniter, which emits primarily in the infrared and visible spectrum.  However, the combination of igniter settings and PMT gains can lead to false ignition detection. 

ACME’s Mass Flow Controllers (MFCs) used to control the flow of fuel, nitrogen, and synthetic air (i.e., 21% oxygen with a balance of nitrogen, on a volume basis) have unfortunately not always performed adequately.  The fuel and especially nitrogen flows sometimes oscillate. Furthermore, the MFC used for the ‘air’ coflow behaves as if it is sticking in a few places, where the flow rate won’t linearly ramp when commanded (as required for the CLD Flame experiment).  It tends to stick in some places and then abruptly overshoot where that has sometimes been seen to extinguish the flame.  As a result, mass flow controllers are tested - without flames - after they are first installed.  Adjustments are often then made to (1) the settings that control when the valves open and (2) the Proportional-Integral-Derivative (PID) settings.  This activity has involved significant testing, separate from flame tests, and has taken place during CLD Flame operations on 17333, 17334, 17347, 17354, 18008, 18022, 18038, 18046, 18143, and 18241.  In this regard, there are an assortment of MFCs with different flow ranges for each of the three gases.  While the MFC tuning

has been beneficial in some cases, it has not eliminated all gas flow problems.  It was seen that nitrogen oscillations tend to damp with time, so a nitrogen pre-flow was implemented so that the oscillations would typically end before the flame measurements.  That approach has been partially successful and was introduced during E-FIELD Flames’ first round.  Later, a procedure of re-zeroing the MFCs prior to each test was also implemented during E-FIELD Flames’ second round. The final day of testing during E-FIELD Flame’s first round was conducted with highly sooty ethylene flames and a positively charged mesh, which tends to push the flame down toward the burner.  A significant amount of soot deposited on the burner, both on the inner tube from which the fuel issues as well as the honeycomb in the burner annulus from which the air flows.  The extent of the soot wasn’t appreciated until flame testing for CLD Flame’s second round began on May 25 (18145).  Additional evaluation tests were performed on May 31 (18151), and the decision was made to have the crew clean the soot from the burner because it was clear that the gas flow from the burner was affected by the soot buildup.  A cleaning procedure was developed, approved, and carried out by astronaut Ricky Arnold on June 19 (18170). The cleaned burner was imaged with the CIR cameras on June 21 (18172), but the evaluation testing was deferred because of a cable mating issue.  The cable mating was corrected by Arnold on June 25 (18176) and the evaluation testing of the cleaned burner was conducted on the following day, June 26 (18177).  Regular flame testing in CLD Flame’s second round finally commenced on July 5 (18186), six weeks after the soot accumulation was identified as an issue.  However, useful scientific results were obtained on the June 26 evaluation day, so the burner cleaning effectively led to a 1- month delay. 

##### **C. Completeness/Quality of Data** 

The number of flames ignited significantly exceeds the planned tests which were (1) specified as required in the ACME Integrated Science Requirements Document (ISRD), using the gas-jet burner, and (2) later expanded to include tests with the coflow burner to partially make up for science loss resulting from the slow response time of the highvoltage power supply.  Following the revision, a total of 74 E-FIELD Flames tests were identified as required.  In contrast, 120 flames were ignited in the experiment’s first round, i.e., first half.  In that round, roughly 200% more flames were ignited than had been planned. 

It should be understood that a complete set of image data is not available for all tests.  As discussed in section III.B, the IPSU-1 was not working at the beginning of the second round of testing, preventing use of both the HiBMs camera and the CIR’s collimated illumination package.  Those are not critical for E-FIELD Flames, so tests continued without their use.

Furthermore, recorded image data is sometimes deleted without downlinking.  This is done because of operational constraints which limit the amount of data that can be managed per test day.  Given the constraint, the decision is sometimes made to sacrifice some image data (e.g., from the HiBMs camera) to allow additional tests to be conducted. This is intentionally done in order to maximize the scientific return of each day of testing within the project constraints. 

Data quality is of course dependent on the experimental hardware.  But neglecting that limitation on data quality, the cameras are sensitive to radiation where pixels gradually become ‘hot’ over time.  While these bright spots are addressed in image post-processing, they are a degradation of the image quality. 

It is possible that the tests are subtly affected by the acceleration environment, but there is not obvious indication of this and it has thus far not been evaluated with available measurements from the Space Acceleration Measurement System (SAMS).  In this regard, note that the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR), in which the experiment is conducted, is equipped with a Passive Rack Isolation System (PaRIS). 

Regardless of the shortcomings discussed above, a great wealth of experimental data has been secured and E-FIELD Flames is considered to be highly successful. 

##### **D. Status of Data Analysis** 

Data from the E-FIELD Flames experiments includes video recordings from the operations camera for general flame behavior, the ACME data camera for high resolution images at various exposures and frame rates as dictated by the flame luminosity, intensified camera images and non-imaging data at 100 Hz.  The non-imaging data is extracted using Matlab scripts (modified from those developed by CLD Flame researchers) to show trends of ion current and flame luminosity as a function of the applied field, and the flame parameters (fuel, flowrate, burner type).  Image data is processed using the OMA software (also developed and used by CLD Flame researchers). 

The overall data volume is large with a storage limit of 127,000 MB per test day, the data has been downloaded to the hard drive with a backup system at UC Irvine.  At this stage, with operations ongoing, the analysis is comparative and qualitative, just to show the major differences in behavior in microgravity and normal-gravity (1-g) flames. 

##### **E. Preliminary Research Findings** 

Results from the early E-FIELD Flame experiments have demonstrated clear differences between normal-gravity and microgravity flame behaviors.  A few examples include the distinct maximum of ion current for co-flow burner flames in microgravity, where the 1-g flames more smoothly reach a saturation condition.  The results also show that the electric body force can produce flame oscillations similar to those driven by buoyancy in normal gravity, and microgravity flame shapes with an electric field can mimic normal-

Burner type plays a role in the flame/field interaction, and in particular, reversing the field polarity for the jet flame does not produce an inverted result over the entire voltage range tested.  Results also demonstrate the interactions between electric fields and soot are more complex in microgravity.  At the same time, the preliminary findings suggest that some of the key expectations generally hold (e.g., ion current scales approximately with flow rate for a fixed fuel and burner).

The key measurement for the E-FIELD Flames experiments is the ion current as a function of applied voltage, i.e., the voltage-current or V-I curve.  This curve provides information on the flames ability to produce chemi-ions based on its saturation ion current, and it provides a measure of combustion performance in that ion production is a function of carbon influx into the flame and the vigorousness of the combustion process.  More compact, higher temperature flames produce more chemi-ions than do larger more diffuse reaction zones of lower overall temperature for the same fuel flow.  The first effect (i.e., the relationship between carbon influx and peak ion current) can be seen in Figure 2, where the flow of 15 cm/s methane produces slightly above 2 microamps of ion current at the peak (1.5 kV/cm) and the flow of 19 cm/s methane has a peak of 2.75 microamps. While the ratio is not exact, the peak ion current is nearly proportional to the flow

- (1) The ion current reaches a peak at approximately the same field strength for both velocities in 1-g while in microgravity the ion current peak for a lower fuel velocity occurs at a lower field strength.  The field strength at the peak current seems to scale linearly with the fuel velocity. 



**Figure 5.** Ion current as a function of field strength and fuel velocity with no coflow in normal gravity. (a) 16 cm/s methane. (b) 19 cm/s methane. 

- (2) In microgravity, the ion current does not smoothly increase to saturation nor does it show a gentle peak with a mild decrease as is seen in the normal gravity results. Instead, in microgravity there is a distinct peak in the ion current with a severe decrease throughout the saturation condition, with a final saturation plateau observed at voltages substantially above the voltage at peak ion current.  That is, the microgravity final saturation is not the maximum ion current observed.  This difference is more dramatic when there is no coflow, which suggests that entrainment plays an important role whether caused by buoyancy or a forced coflow. 

- (3) The peak ion current is higher for microgravity than comparison 1-g flames.  The reason is not obvious at this point but may result from a more focused upward flow from the electric body force and ion-driven wind as compared to the combined effects of the ion wind and buoyancy. 

- (4) The peak ion current is approximately proportional to fuel flow velocity, which, as described in prior studies, reflects the relationship between ion production and the carbon flux into the system.  The peak ion current also increases with the coflow which suggests that healthier oxidizer transport helps increase the ion production.

#### **I. Introduction** 

The Electric-Field Effects on Laminar Diffusion Flames (E-FIELD Flames) experiment was conducted in the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR) on the International Space Station, as part of the Advanced Combustion via Microgravity Experiments (ACME) project.  In this experiment, an electric field with voltages as high as 10,000 volts was established between the burner and a flat, circular mesh electrode suspended above (i.e., downstream of) the burner.  The motion of the charged ions, which are naturally produced within the flame, are strongly affected by a strong electric field.  The resulting ion-driven wind can dramatically influence the stability and sooting behavior of the flame.  Measurements were made of electric-field strength, the ion current passing through the flame, and flame characteristics such as the size, structure, temperature, soot, and stability.  Conducting the tests in microgravity allowed for great simplifications in the 

58 

ACME/E-FIELD Flames Final Report (2019) 

10 April 2021 

analysis of ion-driven convection and flame interaction, enabling new understanding and the development of less polluting and more efficient combustion technology for use on Earth. 

An electric field can strongly influence flames because of its effect on the ions produced as a result of the combustion reactions. The induced ion wind can modify the flame shape, alter the soot and stability limits, and direct the heat transfer.  The purpose of the E-FIELD Flames experiment is to gain an improved understanding of flame ion production and learn how the electric field can be used to manipulate the ions and control non-premixed flames for practical benefit.  For example, electric fields can enable stable burner operation at fuel-lean conditions with reduced pollutant emission, i.e., where the flame is not reliably sustained without the electric field.  As another example, the role of electric fields on soot formation can be dramatic. It appears that the ion driven wind alters the local convection and strongly influences the residence time of the soot precursors within the hot flame.  The experiment contributes to our critical understanding of combustion processes in the presence of electric fields. 

More specifically, E-FIELD Flames has the following experimental objectives: 

- A. Determine the relationship between electric field voltage and chemi-ion current as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and the amount of inert (nitrogen) in the fuel. 

- B. Obtain the time response of the flame chemiluminescence, luminosity, and the ion current to large step changes in voltage, as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and amount of inert (nitrogen) added to the fuel stream. 

- C. Evaluate and manipulate sooting behavior (i.e., soot volume fraction and location of luminous soot) in relation to the electric field voltage and chemi-ion current as functions of total fuel (methane and ethylene) flow rate and amount of inert (nitrogen) added to the fuel stream. 

- D. Use the variation of the chemi-ion current as a function of applied voltage to characterize the combustion behavior of near-limit lifted flames and use the associated ion-driven wind to stabilize or reattach these flames. 

The effect of the electric field on the flame is studied through tests conducted with a range of flame and electric field conditions.  The fuel, fuel jet velocity, air flow, electric-field voltage and polarity were the primary parameters of the E-FIELD Flames experiment.  Tests were conducted with methane and ethylene, including nitrogen dilutions of each, which provide a broad range of soot-producing conditions, i.e., from soot-free to highly sooting.  They were carried out with both a simple gas-jet burner and the coflow burner that was also used for the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment.  Tests with the coflow burner were conducted with and without a coflow of air, where the results of the latter are compared with those from the gas-jet burner to assess the effect of air entrainment on the flame.  The coflow burner also provided a different electrode configuration (close to a plane-to-plane) than the jet burner (close to a point-to-plane). In a typical test, the mesh electrode was energized at increasing potentials (relative to the burner), where the flame’s response (e.g., ion current, luminosity, shape) to the resulting field strength was measured.  The field strength was changed in repeated steps or was instead smoothly ramped.

Microgravity studies of flames allow for a range of simplifications and provide various advantages over normal-gravity studies.  For example, the natural flicker of flames, which is a buoyancydriven (i.e., gravity-driven) instability, is eliminated yielding quasi-steady flames in microgravity. Therefore, the flicker in the E-FIELD Flames was a result of electric body forces alone.  Length scales are increased in microgravity flames facilitating analysis of the flame structure. Momentum-dominated flames, which are important for most practical combustion, can be studied at low velocities to simplify analysis.  Microgravity flames tend to have a much stronger sensitivity to their atmosphere and exhibit a much broader range of characteristics than normalgravity flames because of the near absence of buoyant entrainment.  The long residence times in microgravity flames can lead to strong soot production, but many microgravity flames are soot free.  The absence of buoyancy makes microgravity flames advantageous for studies of limit and stability behavior where chemical kinetics are important.  For example, chemi-ion species, electronically excited chemiluminescent species, are at very low concentration in typical flames, making the reduced buoyancy environment valuable for evaluating the minor species chemistry. With these advantages, microgravity testing can lead to new understanding of the complex nature of combustion – regardless of the gravitational environment of the combustion application. 

#### **II. Methods/Research Operations** 

##### **A. Discussion of Method/Protocol** 

E-FIELD Flames and other ACME experiments are configured, but not conducted, by the ISS crew.  The crew’s role is to set up the hardware, gas bottles, etc. for each set of tests. The most common changes include the gas bottles on the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR) and the mass flow controllers on the modular ACME chamber insert. 

All testing is commanded from the ground, specifically from the Glenn ISS Payload Operations Center (GIPOC).  The science portion of the testing is typically conducted in a nominally automated mode using pre-programmed scripts.  However, it may alternately be conducted in a ‘manual’ mode, where changes are made to a control parameter in response to the analog video downlink and the numerical data available in the telemetry stream.  For example, the mesh voltage can be adjusted to approach a flame’s stability or soot limit.  This was not done during Increments 55-56 or 57-58, where the console commanding during a test was typically limited to halting the test when it was observed in the analog ops video that the flame had extinguished. 

Testing is not conducted when thrusters fire or spacecraft dock or undock from ISS to ensure an acceptable acceleration environment. 

Data is downlinked between each set of tests, i.e., day of testing, to allow for preliminary analysis and requisite planning for subsequent testing.

#### **III. Results** 

##### **A. Baselined Activities Completed – Pre-flight, In-flight, Post-Flight** 

A total of 219 flames were ignited for the E-FIELD Flames experiment, with 142 and 77 respectively ignited during Increments 55-56 and 57-58.  Note that the investigation’s testing was completed during Increment 57, so no flames were ignited during Increment 58.  The first and second rounds of testing respectively occurred in March-May 2018 and September-November 2018.  The two rounds of tests resulted in 120 and 99 ignited flames conducted with coflow and gas-jet burners, respectively.  The coflow burner was developed for the Coflow Laminar Diffusion Flame (CLD Flame) experiment, where CLD Flames’ second round occurred between the two rounds of the E-FIELD Flames experiment.  The ‘interruption’ in the E-FIELD Flames testing was intentionally planned years in advance to enable the PI team to review the initial results in detail and with that knowledge plan the second round of tests to maximize its scientific outcome. 

E-FIELD Flames does not have living subjects like a life science experiment, but the tests are independently conducted with two different gaseous fuels, methane and ethylene. Both are simple hydrocarbon gases for which the chemistry is well understood.  Methane (CH4) is the primary component of natural gas and tends to produce low levels of soot given the absence of carbon-carbon bonds.  Meanwhile, ethylene (C2H4), is highly sooting because of its carbon double bond.  For both fuels, tests were conducted with pure fuel and fuel mixed with nitrogen over a broad range of dilutions.  For limited cases, the fuel and nitrogen was premixed on the ground prior to launch.  ACME bottles were launched with methane concentrations of 30%, 40%, 45%, and 100% with a balance of nitrogen (on a volume basis).  Similarly, bottles were launched with ethylene concentrations of 30%, 50%, and 100%, again with a balance of nitrogen (on a volume basis).  But the ACME hardware also allows for simultaneous flow of fuel and nitrogen to the burner, allowing for on-orbit fuel dilution and testing at a continuum of fuel concentrations.  In all cases, the coflow was 21% oxygen with a balance of nitrogen (on a volume basis). 

No samples (e.g., of soot) were collected for E-FIELD Flames; all of the ISS results were electronically transmitted to the ground.  That is furthermore the case for all currently planned ACME experiments.

**4. Any other difficulties in obtaining scientific data** 

E-FIELD Flames tests sometimes automatically aborted (due to the controlling software script), where that was typically prior to gas flow and the activation of the igniter.  As a result, there was little lost other than the time in the failed test attempt. The aborts normally occurred because of communication issues with IPSU-1.  These are infrequent, but there are some days when tests aborted repeatedly. 

Tests are of course also unsuccessful when the flame fails to ignite.  The first test of the day has sometimes failed to ignite, presumably because the fuel line has not been sufficiently primed.  Ignition was sometimes unsuccessful because the settings for the automatic flame detection are such that detection threshold is reached because of the igniter glow rather than a flame.  As a result of the detection, the hot-wire igniter is deactivated and retracted, which is of course an issue if the flame has yet to ignite. This is typically avoided by detecting ignition with measurement of the flame’s OH* chemiluminescence with a Photomultiplier Tube (PMT) module.  The OH* chemiluminescence, in the ultraviolet at 310 nm, is a good indicator of an ignited flame rather than the glowing igniter, which emits primarily in the infrared and visible spectrum.  However, the combination of igniter settings and PMT gains can lead to false ignition detection. 

ACME’s Mass Flow Controllers (MFCs) used to control the flow of fuel, nitrogen, and synthetic air (i.e., 21% oxygen with a balance of nitrogen, on a volume basis) have unfortunately not always performed adequately. It tends to stick in some places and then abruptly overshoot where that has sometimes been seen to extinguish the flame. ##### **D. Status of Data Analysis** 

Data from the tests making up the E-FIELD Flames experiment includes video recordings from the operations camera for general flame behavior, high resolution images from the ACME data camera at various exposures and frame rates as dictated by the flame luminosity, intensified camera images, and non-imaging data at 125 Hz.  The non-imaging data file is processed using Matlab scripts (modified from those developed by CLD Flame researchers) to separately show trends of ion current and flame luminosity as a function of the applied field, and the flame parameters (fuel, flowrate, burner type).  The PMT data is also extracted to evaluate flame luminosity oscillations.  Image data is processed using the OMA software (also developed and used by CLD Flame researchers). 

The overall data volume is large with a storage limit of 127,000 MB per test day.  All of the data has been downloaded to the hard drive with a backup system at UC Irvine.  At this stage, the data has been sorted into appropriate segments so that comparisons can be made between burner geometries (jet versus coflow from the prior round of testing), fuel type and dilution (methane versus ethylene; no dilution, modest dilution, high dilution) and fuel flow velocity.  The fuel flow velocity and dilution conditions were sometimes selected to hold total carbon flux into the flame constant.  At this stage, the analysis remains comparative and qualitative, to show the major differences in behavior in microgravity and normal-gravity (1-g) flames, and to show particularly how buoyancy modifies the electrical response of the flames. 

##### **E. Preliminary Research Findings** 

Distinctly different results were seen in E-FIELD Flames’ second round tests with the gasjet burner when compared with the first round tests with the coflow burner.  For example, the ion current response to the electric field strength with an upward ion driven wind in microgravity is a clear parabolic trend (which is similar to the trend observed in 1-g flames with the coflow burner as presented in the prior report).  The parabolic behavior is evident even with the strong oscillations observed during the rise.  This rising trend is not observed with the coflow burner. 

The behavior of the soot and its charge state are complicated since in the regions of high field strength, particularly near the fuel tube where the field lines converge, soot can emit or collect free charges.  Hence, the soot transport is driven directly by the field and by the ion-driven wind (and thermophoresis) which means it can travel differentially to the bulk flow.  These various transport elements can push the soot through the flame to burnout, hold it in stasis in the flame for additional growth, or drive it to the burner where it accumulates.  More work is needed to sort through these elements.

In all of the i-v curves, the ion current data points at each field strength stretch across ~0.1 A for each step, which are the jagged features from the spikes following a voltage step change that are mostly associated with the transient capacitance of the system when a rapid voltage step is requested. This report shows the initial results of the E-FIELD Flames experiment using the gas-jet burner in the International Space Station (ISS).  The positive field allows the soot to concentrate and compresses the flame into a high current density thermal source.  The flames demonstrate an unpredicted oscillation before extinguishing with increasing field strength.  This oscillating phenomenon was not observed during the tests with the coflow burner from the first round, with the exception of a case that introduced the airflow at 30 cm/s which increased the surface reaction while the flame tended to balance between electric field effects, ions, and charged soot. The negative field allows the flame to maintain its spherical characteristic in microgravity. The yellow soot appears for both of the electric field directions within the flame around the tip and disappears with increasing field strength after reaching the current peak.  The role of soot in the i-v behavior of these flames requires further investigation.

The Advanced Combustion Microgravity Experiment ( **ACME** ) investigation is a set of studies of gaseous flames to be conducted in the Combustion Integration Rack ( **CIR** ), one of which being Electric-Field Effects on Laminar Diffusion Flames ( **E-FIELD Flames** ). 

In E-FIELD Flames, an electric field with voltages as high as 10,000 volts is established between the burner and a mesh electrode. The motion of the charged ions, which are naturally produced within the flame, are strongly affected by a high-voltage electric field. The resulting ion-driven wind can dramatically influence the stability and sooting behavior of the flame. Measurements are made of electric-field strength, the ion current passing through the flame, and flame characteristics such as the size, structure, temperature, soot, and stability. Conducting the tests in microgravity enables new understanding and the development of less polluting and more efficient combustion technology for use on Earth. 

This week marks the first week of E-FIELD flames operations with a very successful first set of ten flames ignited for studying the effects of an electric field on laminar diffusion flames. Preliminary results show that the high-voltage electric field created forces similar to buoyancy. This effect dramatically affected the flame’s shape, intensity, and soot production.

#### **Appendix J. Summary of the ISS data collection** 

The E-FIELD Flames data is all digital and includes both image and non-image data, where this is true for the other current ACME experiments.  To be clear, there is no collection of soot or other physical samples that are subsequently transported to the ground. 

The E-FIELD Flames image data includes images from the ACME DataCam, the CFI intensified camera (used with the CH* filter), and the High-Bitdepth Multispectral (HiBMs) camera (equipped with a red filter corresponding to the opposing CIR illumination package).  The DataCam images are in a Halcon Iconic Object (hobj) format, while the images from the CFI and HiBMs cameras are in a Tagged Image File (tif) format.  In contrast, the analog video was recorded on the ground in a Transport Stream (ts) format. 

The key non-imaging data from a test is a comma-delimited file that is described in detail in the Software Interface Design Description (ACME-DOC-010 Rev. D). 

The data is currently archived at NASA and work is underway to make it available publicly through NASA’s Physical Science Informatics System (PSI).
