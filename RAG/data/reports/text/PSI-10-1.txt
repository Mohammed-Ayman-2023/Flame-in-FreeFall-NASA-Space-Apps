|The following is a summary of the research activities during the period Oct. 2006 - Sept. 2009.<br>1) Numerical and Experimental Observations of Spherical Diffusion Flames. Spherical diffusion flames supported on a<br>porous sphere were studied numerically and experimentally. Experiments were performed in 2.2 s and 5.2 s microgravity<br>facilities. Numerical results were obtained from a Chemkin-based program. The program simulates flow from a porous<br>sphere into a quiescent environment, yields both steady state and transient results and accounts for optically thick<br>gas-phase radiation. The low flow velocities and long residence times in these diffusion flames lead to enhanced radiative<br>and diffusive effects. Despite similar adiabatic flame temperatures, the measured and predicted temperatures varied by as<br>much as 700 K. The temperature reduction correlates with flame size but characteristic flow times and Lewis number<br>also influence temperature. The numerical results show that the ambient gas Lewis number would have a strong effect<br>on flame temperature if the flames were steady and nonradiating. For example, a 10% decrease in Lewis number would<br>increase the steady state flame temperature by 200 K. However, for these transient, radiating flames the effect of Lewis<br>number is small. It was also observed that when hydrocarbon fuel is supplied from the ambient the large diffusion<br>distances associated with these flames can lead to unusual steady state compositions near the outer boundary because<br>decomposition products can diffuse to the outer boundary. This results in a loss of chemical enthalpy from the system<br>but the effect on flame temperature is small. Transient predictions of flame sizes are larger than those observed in<br>microgravity experiments. Close agreement could not be obtained without either increasing the model’s thermal and<br>mass diffusion properties by 30% or reducing mass flowrate by 25%.|
|2) Radiative Extinction of Gaseous Spherical Diffusion Flames in Microgravity. Radiative extinction of spherical<br>diffusion flames was investigated experimentally and numerically. The experiments involved microgravity spherical<br>diffusion flames burning ethylene and propane at 0.98 bar. Both normal (fuel flowing into oxidizer) and inverse<br>(oxidizer flowing into fuel) flames were studied, with nitrogen supplied to either the fuel or the oxygen. Flame<br>conditions were chosen to ensure that the flames extinguished within the 2.2 s of available test time; thus extinction<br>occurred during unsteady flame conditions. Diagnostics included color video and thin-filament pyrometry. The<br>computations, which simulated flow from a porous sphere into a quiescent environment, included detailed chemistry,<br>transport, and radiation and yielded transient results. Radiative extinction was observed experimentally and simulated<br>numerically. Extinction time, peak temperature, and radiative loss fraction were found to be independent of flow rate<br>except at very low flow rates. Radiative heat loss was dominated by the combustion products downstream of the flame<br>and was found to scale with flame surface area, not volume. For large transient flames the heat release rate also scaled<br>with surface area and thus the radiative loss fraction was largely independent of flow rate. Peak temperatures at<br>extinction onset were about 1100 K, which is significantly lower than for kinetic extinction. An important observation of<br>this work is that while radiative heat losses can drive transient extinction, this is not only because radiative losses are<br>increasing with time but also because the heat release rate is falling off as the flame expands away from the burner and<br>the reactant supply to the flame decreases.|
