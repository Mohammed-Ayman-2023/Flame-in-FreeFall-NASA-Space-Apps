### Project Overview 

This research provides quantitative correlations between solidification and flow characteristics, processing conditions, microstructure, and porosity for solder joints produced in terrestrial vs. microgravity conditions (onboard the International Space Station (ISS)).  Furthermore, this project focuses on analyzing solder joint properties under extreme conditions of elevated and cryogenic temperatures in space. Soldering is the predominant method used to create electrical/mechanical joints and mechanical/pressure joints and is typically a rapid process in which the solder material is molten for only a few seconds. Solder joint porosity is a common but undesirable feature naturally arising from the use of fluxes and is more insidious in soldering joints formed under microgravity conditions. Under the presence of gravity, voids and bubbles are removed from the solder joints due to presence of buoyancy force and vigorous liquid solder mixing. In the _absence_ of gravity, fluid motion is greatly reduced, driven mainly by solidification volume change, thermally induced density gradients, and Marangoni effects. With slower mixing, voids and bubbles are not swept away and become entrapped in the interior of the solder joint upon solidification.  This entrapped porosity dramatically degrades the thermal and electrical properties of the solder and severely reduces the mechanical integrity of the joint. Therefore, as part of the ongoing endeavors to establish an electronics and mechanical joint repair capability for extended space missions, this project seeks to advance the current understanding of fundamental mechanisms, phenomenology, and process conditions that govern the integrity and performance of solder joints produced in terrestrial vs. reduced gravity environments. Additionally, solder joints in the space environment are subjected to large temperature variations such as – 120 ºC to +25 ºC for the Mars missions, and – 157 ºC to +121 ºC outside ISS. However, as deep space exploration missions become longer and more distant, the solder and other metal joints' current reliability is inadequate for the harsh conditions they encounter. 

This project focused on the following objectives to address the reliability of solder joints in space: 1) Characterizing the effects of the lack of Earth's natural convective flow and buoyancy on solderjoint formation onboard the ISS using the 40Pb-60Sn solders from the In-Space Soldering Investigation (ISSI), 2) Investigating the effects of elevated and cryogenic temperature conditions in space on solder properties. Considering a range of potential applications and materials, three solder alloys were investigated, including the ISSI lead-based (40Pb-60Sn) solders, off-eutectic 50Pb-50Sn solders, and lead-free (95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu) solders, which had recently shown promise for high-performance joint applications due to their good thermal and electrical conductivities, as well as excellent corrosion and fatigue resistance. 

Our team conducted the microstructural characterization of four microgravity-processed solders from the ISSI study, one ISSI terrestrial solder, and a third set of freshly made terrestrial 40wt%Pb60wt%Sn solder to demonstrate the effects of the absence of buoyancy and natural convection on void formation in microgravity. The effect of void-induced Marangoni convection on the solder microstructure was demonstrated using the microgravity wire-feed solder. Additionally, the micromechanical response of solders processed in terrestrial and microgravity environments was investigated under extreme elevated and cryogenic temperature conditions (ranging from –157ºC to +121ºC, as experienced outside the ISS). Under cryogenic conditions, the study investigated the impact of the β-to-α phase transformation in Sn below 13ºC, including associated volume changes, internal stress development, and the ductile-to-brittle transition observed in various Sn-based 

3 

solder alloys. At room temperature, the effects of accelerated aging on the solders' microstructure and mechanical properties were explored. 

### Major Goals/Objectives 

The major focus of this project was to elucidate the fundamental mechanisms, phenomenology, and process conditions that govern the integrity and performance of solder joints produced in terrestrial vs. reduced gravity environments, such as the microgravity conditions onboard the International Space Station (ISS). The primary objectives included advancing the current qualitative understanding of porosity formation and persistence in solder joints into the realm of alloy/process-specific quantitative description and prediction and evaluating the influence of elevated and cryogenic temperatures on the microstructural and mechanical properties of solder joints, with emphasis on Sn-based alloys. 

Leveraging solder samples from the In-Space Soldering Investigation (ISSI) and other non-ISSI compositions, the project employed a combination of advanced experimental techniques and computational modeling. These approaches were designed to characterize the impacts of voidinduced microstructural variations, extreme thermal cycling, and phase transformations, addressing critical reliability challenges for soldering in space environments. The project aimed to address critical scientific gaps related to solder joint reliability in the unique microgravity and thermal conditions of space, with the ultimate goal of providing quantitative insights for designing more robust soldering techniques. While this proposal focused on understanding the fundamental phenomena, it also laid a solid scientific foundation for future advancements in space fabrication and repair technologies. This work contributes directly to NASA’s goals of improving in-space manufacturing and repair capabilities while establishing a strong foundation for future innovations in soldering processes tailored to the harsh conditions of deep-space exploration.

### Accomplishments under Goals 

The research program achieved several significant accomplishments in advancing the understanding of solder behavior in terrestrial vs. microgravity environments, with a focus on enhancing their reliability for space applications. This study analyzed near-eutectic 40Pb-60Sn solder samples from the In-Space Soldering Investigation (ISSI), conducted in microgravity aboard the International Space Station (ISS) and under terrestrial conditions at NASA Marshall. The ISS experiments were performed by taking pieces of silver-coated copper wire and bending and twisting them into multiple test coupon configurations. The selected lengths of 40wt%Pb60wt%Sn wire were cut and 1) wrapped around the test coupons and melted using the soldering iron – Wire wrap solder and 2) fed on the similar test coupons that were heated by the soldering iron – Wire feed solder (Fig.1). The ground experiments involved conductively melting 10 cm lengths of 40Pb-60Sn (3.3 wt% rosin flux) solder onto a loop turned into the silver coated copper wire. For more details about the ISSI experiments, refer to: Background: In-Space Soldering Investigation (ISSI 2003-04) section in this report. 

Additionally, near-eutectic 40Pb-60Sn, off-eutectic 50Pb-50Sn, and 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu terrestrial solder samples (named ISU terrestrial) were prepared and examined in Dr. Pathak’s research lab at Iowa State University, Ames. For a detailed list of samples and tasks, refer to the Outline of Executive Summary of this report. Key accomplishments include: 

Accomplishments from the study on near-eutectic 40Pb-60Sn solder (microgravity and terrestrial): Samples analyzed: 

   - ISSI microgravity 40Pb-60Sn wire warp solder (Microgravity test 2 sample 4, 5 and 10) 

   - ▪ ISSI microgravity 40Pb-60Sn wire feed solder (Microgravity test 4 sample 16 – MT4S16) ▪ ISSI terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn wire feed solder 

   - ISU Terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn wire wrap and wire feed solder (prepared in Dr. Pathak’s lab) 

   - ▪ ISU Terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn Reflow Spreading solder (prepared in Dr. Pathak’s lab) 

- Demonstrated the effect of natural convection and buoyancy on void size in  40Pb-60Sn solder formed under microgravity vs. terrestrial conditions using non-destructive 3-D tomography. Results demonstrated that the occurrence of larger voids (as large as 1 mm in diameter) only happens in microgravity conditions. 

- Developed a quantitative framework for describing and predicting porosity formation and persistence in 40Pb-60Sn solder under microgravity vs. terrestrial conditions, addressing the role of reduced buoyancy-driven convection and flux volatilization. ISSI microgravity wire wrap 40Pb-60Sn solder had ~13× more voids than ISSI terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn solder, while ISSI microgravity wire feed 40Pb-60Sn solder had 4× more voids than ISSI wire wrap 40Pb60Sn solder. 

- Studied the effect of Marangoni convection in microgravity 40Pb-60Sn solder under the presence of a large free surface (large void, greater than 1 mm) on solder microstructure (primary dendrite and small void accumulation). The average Pb-particle size near the hot site was 6.1 ± 5 µm and near the cold site (expected to be at a lower temperature than the hot site) was 4.8 ± 5 µm which was caused by the differential cooling rate in the colder and hot sites and accumulation of Pb-rich dendrite in the hot region. 

- Quantified the effect of microstructure, influenced by void-induced Marangoni convection, on microgravity solder properties. We demonstrated that Inhomogeneity (accumulation of Pb-rich 

5 

dendrites at the solder joint) resulting from Marangoni flow caused lower strength (hardness ~ 0.11 GPa) of the solder at the hot site than at the colder site (hardness ~ 0.15 GPa). 

- The aging study on terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn solder showed a hardness decrease from 0.19 GPa on day 1 to 0.16 GPa on day 483, accompanied by Pb particle growth from 1.28 μm to 2.45 μm over the same period. 

- Investigated the β-Sn to α-Sn phase transformation below 13°C on pure Sn single crystal and terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn solder using high-throughput spherical nanoindentation, elucidating the effect of associated volume changes and internal stresses on mechanical performance in cryogenic environments. 

- Investigated the effects of ductile to brittle transition on terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn solder properties via in-situ SEM micropillar compressions. Plastic strain at instability under compression in 40Pb-60Sn decreased from ɛ = 0.109 at RT to ɛ = 0.06 below -85°C, suggesting a ductile-tobrittle transition near this temperature. 

Accomplishments from the study of terrestrial off-eutectic 50Pb-50Sn solder: Sample analyzed: 

   - ISU Terrestrial 50Pb-50Sn solder sample 1 to 3 (prepared in Dr. Pathak’s lab) 

- Aging study on terrestrial 50Pb-50Sn demonstrated that the hardness dropped from 0.18 to 0.14 GPa, and Pb particle size grew from 0.45 to 0.8 μm over 89 days. 

Accomplishments from the study on terrestrial 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu solder: Sample analyzed: 

   - ISU Terrestrial 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu solder sample 8 (prepared in Dr. Pathak’s lab) 

- In-Situ SEM micro-pillar compression on ISU terrestrial 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu solder demonstrated that plastic strain at instability under compression dropped from ɛ = 0.06 at RT to ɛ = 0.03 at -85°C and lower temperatures, signaling a ductile-to-brittle transition around this temperature. 

- The deformation behavior was correlated with microstructure across all temperatures.  Group 3 (eutectic mixture of Ag3Sn + Sn) demonstrated a sharp decrease in the plastic strain at instability as the temperature drops below room temperature (RT), probably caused by the high fraction of the Ag₃Sn intermetallic phase that significantly influences the mechanical response. At cryogenic temperatures, Ag₃Sn is much stiffer and more brittle than the Sn-rich phase. As temperature decreases, deformation becomes increasingly localized at phase boundaries, reducing plasticity and accelerating the ductile-to-brittle transition. 

- In contrast, Group 2a (eutectic Ag3Sn + Sn + Sn-rich dendrite) showed a gradual decrease in plastic strain with decreasing temperature, as its balanced mix of Sn-rich and eutectic phases allowed for some residual plasticity, unlike the sudden drop observed in Group 3. Group 2b (Cu5Sn6 + group 2a) exhibited the lowest plastic strain across all temperatures due to the presence of Cu₆Sn₅ intermetallic compounds, which increased stiffness and restricted plastic deformation, leading to early instability.

The first year of this project was focused on studying the near-eutectic 40Pb-60Sn (terrestrial and microgravity) and off-eutectic 50Pb-50Sn (terrestrial) solder compositions. This study focused on understanding the role of reduced buoyancy forces and natural convection in void formation and microstructural inhomogeneity in 40Pb-60Sn solder under microgravity conditions, alongside the effects of room temperature aging on ISSI microgravity 40Pb-60Sn, terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn and terrestrial 50Pb-50Sn solder properties. Additionally, the study aimed to quantify the influence of Marangoni flow and thermal convection on void dynamics and phase segregation in ISSI microgravity 40Pb-60Sn solder. The study demonstrated that microgravity conditions significantly increased void fraction (ISSI microgravity wire wrap solder had ~13× more voids than terrestrial solder) and size in solder joints due to the absence of buoyancy-driven convection, with Marangoni flow contributing to void and Pb-rich dendrite accumulation in high-temperature regions. Mechanical testing revealed ductile-to-brittle transitions in ISU terrestrial 40Pb-60 Sn solder under cryogenic conditions at temperatures at and below -85<sup>o</sup> C, while aging studies highlighted grain coarsening (Pb particle size =1.28 μm on day 1 to 2.45 μm on day 483) and hardness reduction (0.19 GPa on day 1 to 0.15 GPa on day 483) in 40Pb-60Sn solder alloys.

The second year of this project focused on 1) quantifying the effects of void-induced Marangoni convection and thermal gradients on ISSI microgravity 40Pb-60Sn solder strength and 2) evaluating the influence of elevated and cryogenic temperatures on Sn-based terrestrial solders (40Pb-60Sn, 50Pb-50Sn and 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu) properties, including ductile-to-brittle transition and phase transformations. 

The study revealed that void-induced Marangoni convection in microgravity significantly reduces solder hardness near high-temperature regions due to the localized accumulation of softer Pb-rich phases, highlighting the critical need to develop soldering processes that mitigate microstructural 

7 

inconsistencies to ensure the structural integrity of solder joints in space environments. Cryogenic temperature studies on ISU terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn solder using high throughput spherical nanoindentation stress-strain analysis revealed that 40Pb-60Sn solder exhibits increased yield strength from 0.18 GPa (0.05 offset yield strength) at RT to 0.32 GPa at -120°C, while pure Sn shows unusual softening at -120°C, likely due to the β-to-α phase transformation, emphasizing the need for alloy-specific reliability assessments under extreme conditions. The micro-pillar compression experiments revealed a significant ductile-to-brittle transition in ISU terrestrial 40Pb60Sn solder below -85°C, characterized by decreased plastic strain at instability from 0.109 at RT to 0.062 at -85°C and deformation concentrated along Pb-rich and Sn-rich phase boundaries. Micropillar compression experiments on ISU terrestrial 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu solder at cryogenic temperatures revealed a significant increase in stress at instability which changed from 78 MPa at RT to 153 MPa at -145°C, with a noticeable saturation in instability stress below -85°C and a reduction in the plastic strain at instability (from 0.06 at RT to 0.03 at -85°C ), primarily attributed to a ductile-to-brittle transition. Deformation mechanisms in 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu varied with temperature and percentage of various phases in the different pillars, with deformation predominantly progressing along Sn-rich dendrites at -40°C, shifting to eutectic and intermetallic interfaces below -85°C, highlighting the critical role of phase boundaries in cryogenic mechanical behavior.

### Project Motivation 

Metal joining (welding, brazing, and soldering) in the space environment provides a solution towards sustainable, flexible missions through on-demand fabrication and repair of sustained damage to the spacecraft, satellites, and habitat structures [1, 2]. Metal joining in space is subjected to: a) reduced gravity, b) reduced pressure, and c) a large temperature gradient than terrestrial metal joining. Once the metal joints are produced, they experience extreme temperature variations such as – 120ºC to +25ºC for Mars missions, – 230ºC to +120ºC for the missions to Moon, and – 157ºC to +121ºC outside the ISS. Understanding the metal joining parameters in microgravity and joint integrity during welding, brazing, and soldering under extreme temperatures in space is critical for the safety and success of NASA’s space exploration missions to and beyond low Earth orbit. In this proposed research, we primarily focus on (microgravity vs. terrestrial) solder joints because: ● Solder alloys are low melting alloys due to which they experience the largest change in microstructural and mechanical properties under extreme temperatures. ● Solder alloys are an important class of materials for in-space manufacturing and metal joining, as they serve as mechanical and electrical connectors between components and substrates in electronic packaging and are used for mechanical/pressure joints. 

Solder joints in electronic components are routinely subjected to large temperature variations in the space environment. To compensate, long-duration missions such as Mars rover missions have traditionally kept their electronics in a heated enclosure known as a “warm electronics box” (WEB) so that they do not get exposed to temperature ranges beyond –40ºC to +40ºC [3]. This approach of protecting electronics from extreme temperatures reduces the spacecraft payload ratio, increases launch costs, and fails to protect the electronics, such as solar arrays, exposed outside. Therefore, future missions are planning to use a distributed, non-heated architecture for the rover and spacecraft electronics, where the components will be placed near the systems they interact with rather than in a heated enclosure [4]. This new approach relies on low-cost, commercially available, and space-qualified electronic packaging assemblies. With this approach, electronic components, without requiring heater power or WEB, must be capable of operating under the extreme temperature experienced in these longer and more distant deep space exploration missions [5, 6]. 

Addressing the reliability of solder joints under these multi-extreme environments is critical for the success of future deep space exploration missions. Specifically, the solder joints used in onboard electronics must be able to withstand elevated and cryogenic temperatures and large variations in temperature. For example, the ISS orbits Earth 16 times in 24 hours and experiences 16 sunsets and sunrises, due to which anything on the outer surface of the ISS experiences extreme cryo and elevated temperature 16 times in a day. 

### Scientific Questions and Problem Statement 

The first year of this project focused on understanding the factors contributing to increased void accumulation in solder joints prepared under microgravity conditions, specifically the higher number and larger size of voids. For a detailed discussion of these findings, refer to the Year 1 report. Building on this foundation, the second year of the study shifted focus toward assessing the reliability of these solder joints in space environments. 

Solder joints are crucial in electronic packaging, serving as mechanical and electrical connectors between components and substrates. A single failure in these solder joints can lead to the malfunction of the integrated circuit or even the entire device. Notably, solder joint failures are the 

16 

primary cause of 70% of onboard electronic failures attributed to package failure [7-9]. Therefore, to address the solder joint’s reliability, this project focuses on the following question: _How do extreme temperatures, both elevated and cryogenic, experienced by the solder joint in space affect its properties?_ 

Under Cryogenic Temperature: 

- Sn-based solders undergo a ductile to brittle transition (DBT) at the cryo temperature leading to brittle crack formation, which can cause the catastrophic failure of the solder joints. 

- Sn-based solders generally occur a β-Sn to α-Sn phase transition below 13ºC where β- structure (Body-centered tetragonal) transforms to α-structure (Diamond cubic). This phase transformation causes a 26% increase in volume that generates internal stresses, due to which the reliability of the Sn-based solder joints is compromised for space applications. Although there is existing literature on this subject [5, 6, 10], a comprehensive understanding of how these phase transformations influence the solder properties remains elusive. 

The β-Sn to α-Sn transformation is influenced by several factors, including grain size, the presence of specific solutes, and seeding with compatible materials. Coarser grain structures, characterized by fewer heterogeneous nucleation sites such as grain boundaries, corners, and edges, exhibit slower transformation rates. Elements like Pb, Bi, and Sb, which have good solubility in Sn, hinder the transformation by pinning dislocations within the Sn matrix, making lattice expansion energetically unfavorable. In contrast, seeding with materials like α-Sn, InSb, and CdTe, which possess similar lattice parameters, accelerates nucleation and promotes the transformation process [5, 6, 11-15]. Additionally, the finer grains tend to lower the DBTT, while coarser grains raise it. Finer grains provide more grain boundaries, which act as obstacles to crack propagation. Cracks have to change direction repeatedly as they traverse the grain boundaries, requiring more energy and delaying brittle fracture. This helps maintain ductile behavior to lower temperatures, reducing the DBTT [16]. The solder microstructure in a microgravity environment generally exhibits coarser features compared to those formed under terrestrial conditions. This coarsening is primarily due to the altered dynamics of heat 

and mass transfer in the absence of gravitydriven natural convection. Without buoyancy forces, fluid motion is significantly reduced, and heat transfer relies predominantly on diffusion, which is much slower. Consequently, the slower cooling rates allow grains and phases to grow larger, resulting in a coarser microstructure. As a result, the kinetics of the β-Sn to α-Sn transformation are expected to slow down in microgravity, while the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT) is likely to increase. To address these challenges, it is critical to optimize soldering process parameters, alloy composition, and microstructure to inhibit the β-Sn to α-Sn transformation at cryogenic

Background: In-Space Soldering Investigation (ISSI 2003-04) 

This project utilizes the solder samples from the _ISSI experiments_ performed in terrestrial and microgravity conditions (onboard ISS). ISSI experiments aboard the ISS present a unique opportunity to improve our fundamental understanding of the soldering phenomena in a reduced gravity environment. The experimental procedure consisted of 86 soldering experiments conducted on the ISS in 2003-04, and an equivalent set of groundbased experiments using identical equipment and supplies. The ISS experiments were performed by taking pieces of silver-coated copper wire and bending and twisting them into multiple test coupon configurations. The selected lengths of 40wt%Pb60wt%Sn wire were cut and 1) wrapped around the test coupons and melted using the soldering iron 2) fed on the similar test coupons that were heated by the soldering iron (Fig.1). The ground experiments involved conductively melting 10 cm lengths of 40Pb60Sn (3.3 wt% rosin flux) solder onto a loop turned into the silver coated copper wire as well as straight silver coated copper wire.

Results from the ISSI experiments performed aboard the ISS have shown that soldering in microgravity is expected to be considerably different than their ground-based counterparts due to Earth's natural convective flow and buoyancy effects being minimized in microgravity during melting and solidification [21-23]. On Earth, tests showed the solder melting, sagging, dropping, or running down the test wire due to the buoyancy (density) effect (Fig. 2a). In comparison, aboard the ISS, the molten solder was observed to form an equilibrium "football" shape (Fig. 2b). The changes in geometric shape are believed to be due to the dominance of surface tension in microgravity [24-26]. Shortly after equilibrium, the accrued flux was seen spinning around the 

19 

sample's surface. As the solder started melting, the internal core of flux was released. When the solder was nearly melted, the released flux formed a drop and migrated to the bottom of the solder, which was closest to the heat source (soldering iron). Shortly after, the flux droplet started to spin around the molten solder. The rotation rate increased steadily and then reached a plateau. This spinning continued until the heat source was removed, after which the flux drop slowed down and eventually stopped. The variation in flux rotation in three samples was probably because of different temperature gradients imposed by different soldering iron tip positions on Cu wire. The heat is initially transmitted through highly conductive Cu wire, which melts the solder. The solder edge closest to the heat source is the hottest, while the opposite edge is likely the coldest, which causes the temperature gradient across the solder surface. Therefore, flux rotation on the sample surface gave indirect evidence

Also, under typical soldering conditions, rapid reflow temperatures results in flux volatilization and bubble formation. In the presence of gravity, liquid solders tend to mix vigorously during the reflow period due to natural convection. This is beneficial, as any voids or bubbles that form due to evaporated fluxes are quickly swept to free surfaces and are thus removed from the solder joint. However, the situation is worse in the reduced gravity environment of space ( ̴1×10<sup>-5</sup> g) where, due to minimized buoyancy, bubbles have less chance to escape, thus complicating any in-space fabrication and repair operations. The ISSI data has demonstrated that in the _absence_ of gravity, fluid motion is greatly reduced, driven mainly by solidification volume change, thermally induced density gradients, and Marangoni effects. Therefore, due to the lack of buoyancy force and slower mixing in the absence of natural convection, voids, and bubbles are not swept away and become entrapped in the interior of the solder joint upon solidification in microgravity conditions (Fig. 2c vs. d). During the process of soldering, the liquid solder temperature near the substrate, which is in contact with the heater, is higher than that far away from the substrate/heater. Therefore, a distinct surface tension gradient will form with low surface tension in the high-temperature region and high surface tension in the low-temperature region. The effect of such a temperature gradient is predominant in the absence of the buoyancy force and natural convective flow [27]. Then the Marangoni force will tend to pull the voids towards the high-temperature regions such as the substrate.

Task D: Micro-Mechanical Testing Strategies for Solders at Elevated and Cryo Temperatures 

<u>Effect of microstructure, influenced by void-induced Marangoni convection, on microgravity solder properties:</u> 

As discussed in Task C, void-induced Marangoni convection in ISSI microgravity 40Pb-60Sn wire-feed solder drives the accumulation of voids and Pb-rich dendrites toward the hot site. This microstructural variation significantly impacts the mechanical properties of the solder. We performed Berkovich nanoindentation experiments at 1.5 μm indentation depth using the Hysitron TI950 nanoindenter. Nanoindentation analysis of the wire feed solder reveals a reduction in hardness from 0.15 GPa at the cold site to 0.11 GPa at the hot site (Fig. 5), correlating with the localized accumulation of the softer Pb-rich phase near the substrate. This uneven distribution of Pb-rich dendrites and resultant lower hardness at the solder joint, driven by void-induced Marangoni convection, poses a risk to the structural integrity of solder joints during in-space soldering of electronic components. To ensure reliable performance and achieve a homogeneous microstructure, it is crucial to mitigate the effects of void-induced Marangoni convection on void and dendrite accumulation. This understanding underscores the importance of designing soldering processes that minimize these microstructural inconsistencies in microgravity environments.

<u>Investigating the Effect of the cryogenic temperature on β-Sn to α-Sn phase transformation, ductile to brittle transition and resultant properties of the solder joints:    Sn-based solders undergo a ductile</u> to brittle transition (DBT) at the cryo temperature leading to brittle crack formation, which can cause the catastrophic failure of the solder joints. Additionally, Sn-based solders generally undergo a β-Sn to α-Sn phase transition below 13<sup>o</sup> C, where β-structure (Body-centered tetragonal) transforms to α-structure (Diamond cubic).  To investigate the effect of various cryo temperatures (similar to temp conditions on the Moon, Mars, and outside ISS) on solder properties, we performed high-throughput spherical indentation on terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn and pure Sn(111) single crystals at RT and -120<sup>o</sup> C and in-situ SEM micropillar compression on terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn, 50Pb-50Sn, and 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu solder compositions at RT, – 40ºC, – 85ºC, – 120ºC and – 145ºC. These temperatures are selected based on the literature review [5, 6, 10] on Sn phase transformation and solder joint application during space exploration missions. 

Solder properties under extreme temperatures using Spherical indentation: Sample analyzed: Sn(111) single crystal and ISU terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn solder 

To investigate the long-term reliability of the solder joints under various elevated and cryo temperature conditions, we utilized the Hysitron TI 980 nanoindenter to obtain the nanoindentation stress-strain from Sn single crystal and terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn solder at RT and - 120<sup>o</sup> C. The 0.2% offset yield stress, stress at instability, and plastic strain at instability were further categorized based on pillar groups (Fig. 21). Each group exhibited an increase in stress at instability and 0.2% offset yield stress, along with a decrease in plastic strain at instability at cryogenic temperatures. 

- Group 3: The 0.2% offset yield stress increased from 84 MPa at RT to 109 MPa at -145°C, while stress at instability rose from 115 MPa to 166 MPa. Plastic strain dropped sharply from 0.102 at RT to 0.027 at -145°C, with a sudden decrease from RT to -40°C followed by saturation. 

- Group 2a: The 0.2% offset yield stress increased from 57 MPa at RT to 98 MPa at -145°C, while stress at instability rose from 80 MPa to 149 MPa. Plastic strain gradually decreased from 0.064 at RT to 0.02 at -145°C. 

- Group 2b: The 0.2% offset yield stress increased from 26 MPa at RT to 109 MPa at -145°C, while stress at instability rose from 28 MPa to 148 MPa. However, plastic strain at instability in this group showed no significant change at cryogenic temperatures. 

- Since group 1 is only present at -40°C, a direct trend across all temperatures is not observed. 

Group 3 (Ag₃Sn + Sn eutectic) demonstrated the highest stress at instability and 0.2% offset yield stress due to its composite nature and higher fraction of the hard Ag₃Sn intermetallic phase (Fig. 21b, c). The following conclusions can be made from these results: 

35 

- In Group 2a, plastic strain gradually decreases with decreasing temperature. Unlike Group 3, which exhibits a sudden drop, Group 2a has a more gradual decrease due to the balance between the Sn-rich phase and eutectic regions, allowing for some residual plasticity. 

- Group 2b consistently has the lowest plastic strain across all temperatures. This is probably caused by the presence of Cu₆Sn₅ intermetallic compounds, which increase local stiffness and restrict plastic deformation. Cu₆Sn₅ is a hard and brittle phase that limits dislocation movement, making the material prone to early instability. The lack of significant change in strain across temperatures suggests that the material is already in a constrained deformation regime, dominated by intermetallic interfaces rather than ductile phase behavior. 

- Group 3 shows a sharp decrease in the plastic strain at instability as the temperature drops below room temperature (RT). This is probably caused by the high fraction of the Ag₃Sn intermetallic phase that significantly influences the mechanical response. At cryogenic temperatures, Ag₃Sn is much stiffer and more brittle than the Sn-rich phase. As temperature decreases, deformation becomes increasingly localized at phase boundaries, reducing plasticity and accelerating the ductile-to-brittle transition. 

This analysis highlights the importance of understanding localized deformation in solder joints under cryogenic conditions, which is crucial for in-space applications. Depending on the soldering environment (microgravity vs. gravity), solder composition, soldering process parameters, and solder microstructure, the solder behavior can vary under cryogenic temperatures. Such as, The absence of natural convection and reduced fluid motion in microgravity results in a coarser grain structure compared to terrestrial conditions, leading to fewer nucleation sites for the β-Sn to α-Sn transformation and slower phase transformation kinetics. However, the coarser grain structure in microgravity is expected to accelerate the ductile-to-brittle transition due to faster transformation kinetics. To mitigate these effects, the addition of trace alloying elements such as indium, bismuth, and silver can help prevent the β-to-α Sn transformation, while rapid cooling can refine the grain structure, improving mechanical stability. Implementing both strategies together is essential to suppress both the phase transformation and the ductile-to-brittle transition, ensuring improved solder reliability in microgravity environment. 

Summary: 

- This study investigated the 40Pb-60Sn solder sample prepared during in-space soldering investigation in microgravity conditions onboard ISS and terrestrial conditions at NASA Marshall, along with solder samples prepared in Dr. Pathak's lab at Iowa State University Ames (ISU). This study also extended to non-ISSI solders such as off-eutectic 50Pb-50Sn and 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu solders prepared in terrestrial conditions at ISU. 

- The effects of microgravity on solder joint formation, microstructure, and mechanical behavior was investigated using advanced characterization techniques. Non-destructive 3D tomography revealed that larger voids occurred only in microgravity due to the absence of natural convection. A quantitative framework demonstrated that ISSI microgravity 40Pb-60Sn wire wrap solder had ~13× more voids than ISSI terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn solder, while ISSI 40Pb60Sn wire feed solder had 4× more voids than ISSI 40Pb-60Sn wire wrap solder. 

- The study examined Marangoni convection in microgravity, which induced Pb-rich dendrite accumulation in hot regions, lowering hardness (0.11 GPa) at hot sites compared to colder sites (0.15 GPa). Aging studies on terrestrial 40Pb-60Sn and terrestrial 50Pb-50Sn solder showed grain coarsening and hardness reduction, affecting long-term reliability. 

36 

- Cryogenic investigations using in-situ SEM micropillar compression a ductile-to-brittle transition (DBT) in solder. In 40Pb-60Sn, strength increased from 45.9 MPa at RT to 161.4 MPa at -145°C, contradicting the expected β-to-α phase transformation. Additionally, XRD analysis showed no phase transformation. Furthermore, plastic strain dropped from 0.109 at RT to 0.062 at -85°C, saturating below this temperature, with deformation localized at Pb-Sn phase boundaries, confirming DBT. 

- Similarly, in ISU terrestrial 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu solder, plastic strain decreased from 0.06 at RT to 0.04 at -40°C, stabilizing at 0.03 below -40°C, which indicated the effect of ductile to brittle transition. Strength increased significantly, with instability stress rising from 78.1 MPa at RT to 153 MPa at -145°C. 

- All pillar groups in ISU terrestrial 95.5Sn-3.8Ag-0.7Cu solder showed an increase in stress at instability and 0.2% offset yield stress with decreasing temperature, while plastic strain at instability decreased. Group 3 (Ag₃Sn + Sn eutectic) exhibited the highest strength but experienced a sharp drop in plastic strain below room temperature, whereas Group 2a showed a more gradual decline. Group 2b had the lowest plastic strain across all temperatures due to the presence of Cu₆Sn₅ intermetallic compounds, which restricted deformation. 

- The high fraction of Ag₃Sn in Group 3 made it stiffer and more brittle at cryogenic temperatures, leading to localized deformation at phase boundaries and a rapid ductile-tobrittle transition. Group 2b, containing Cu₆Sn₅, exhibited a minimal change in plastic strain, indicating a constrained deformation regime. In contrast, Group 2a, with a mix of Sn-rich and eutectic phases, retained some plasticity, resulting in a more gradual transition. 

- This work on solder behavior at cryogenic temperature establishes a baseline for solder behavior across extreme temperatures and providing insights for future microgravity metal joining experiments on the ISS or other reduced gravity platforms.
